## ONE

The bell rang at three, and Mark was through the side doors before the echo finished dying, backpack on one shoulder, the strap of it already broken in a way he refused to let his father fix because fixing things was what Saturday was for, and Saturday was four days and also quite possibly never.

Happy was waiting at the curb in the big black car, leaning on the hood with his arms folded, pretending he had not been watching the doors for ten minutes.

"Strap's losing," Happy said.

"The strap is winning," Mark said, and got in.

The February sun came through the windshield flat and gold. Happy pulled away from the curb, and they sat in the school traffic, and neither of them said anything for a while, because that was how the good parts of a ride started.

"How was the quiz?" Happy said at the light.

"Geography." Mark dug a crushed bag of chips out of the bottom of the backpack, brushed off the worst of the lint, and ate them anyway. "I knew where Afghanistan was before we studied it. Dad talks about it sometimes. Weapons stuff. The desert part."

"Don't say 'weapons stuff' with your mouth open."

"You can't even see my mouth."

"I can hear it."

They stopped at the burger place on the way home, the one with the red stools, because it was Thursday and Thursday had always been fries-early day, even if nobody had made that a rule out loud. Happy ordered like a man who had eaten there four hundred times. Mark ordered like a kid. Somewhere in the middle of it the TV over the counter went to the entertainment desk, and the woman with the bright hair said the name Stark, and Mark looked up with a fry hanging out of his mouth.

"—the Apogee Award ceremony tonight in Las Vegas, where Tony Stark is expected to receive—"

"Expected," Mark said.

"He'll show," Happy said, with absolutely no belief.

"Five bucks says craps table by nine."

Happy looked at him for a long moment, mournful, like a man watching money leave his wallet early. "I am not betting against your father's attendance. That's a bet I'm not allowed to win."

"So that's a no."

"That's a no."

## TWO

The house was the house. Big and glass and cold in the evenings unless someone lived in it loud, which was Mark's whole job most days.

"Welcome home, Mark," JARVIS said from the walls. "There are no messages requiring your attention."

"That means no messages."

"That means no messages."

He dropped the backpack by the kitchen counter, which was where it lived, and opened his math homework at the bar stool, which was where it died. The notebook stayed in the bag. The notebook came out later, at night, when the day had finished happening. That was just how notebooks worked.

He worked for eleven minutes. Then the TV.

The coverage had started for real now. Cameras on a casino carpet, a podium with flowers, someone in a uniform holding a small polished trophy like it weighed something. Mark ate a bowl of cereal out of the box, standing up, watching Lieutenant Colonel Rhodes stand behind the podium and say careful words about a friend who was not in the building, and the cameras flashed, and the uniform didn't blush but it was close.

He got down two glasses from the cabinet. Poured milk into both, out of habit, from the years of somebody sitting across from him doing the same thing. Looked at the second glass. Put it back in the cabinet unwashed, which was cheating, but nobody was there to count it except JARVIS, and JARVIS didn't count that kind.

"Will my father be home tonight?" Mark asked.

"Mr. Stark's schedule indicates travel tomorrow morning," JARVIS said. "Afghanistan. A demonstration."

"I know where it is. We had a quiz."

"Then you are currently less surprised than the Air Force."

Mark snorted milk out of his nose, which hurt, and was worth it.

## THREE

Las Vegas, that same hour.

The Apogee people had planned for this. You could tell they had planned for this, because the man who ran the ceremony had a sweat at his hairline that started before the music finished, and the empty chair behind the podium had been angled, very slightly, toward the curtain, in the hope the absence might read as an entrance waiting to happen. It did not read that way. It read as an empty chair, the way an empty chair does when everyone in a tuxedo is pretending not to look at it.

Lieutenant Colonel James Rhodes took the stage with the trophy, and did what soldiers do when a plan fails in public: he stood up straighter and carried it himself. He said only that the award belonged to a man who had changed the field they all worked in. He said the man could not be here tonight. He did not say where the man was, because he knew precisely where the man was. Knowing it made his jaw do a small thing once, on camera, that the society pages would not catch.

The casino floor was three walls away, and louder.

Tony Stark was at the craps table with sunglasses on indoors, because the lights in there had never once been kind at the right hour, and because nobody ever tells a man with a Rolls parked out front to take his sunglasses off. He had a stack of chips going the wrong direction and a smile going the right one, and the table loved him the way tables love anyone who loses beautifully.

Rhodes found him by the noise.

He did not yell. Career officers do not yell on casino carpets. He simply arrived at Tony's shoulder with the trophy under his arm like evidence at a trial, and stood there until the man with the dice noticed the shape of his evening changing.

What passed between them then was short and old: the trophy Tony didn't take seriously and would keep anyway, the friend who had driven across the night to hand it to him anyway, and a bet or two more, because a craps table does not release a man mid-roll, not even for the Air Force.

## FOUR

The phone rang at the Malibu house past ten.

Mark knew it was him before he answered, because nobody else got through the house line at that hour — JARVIS had a list, and the list was short, and Pepper's name on it came with different hours.

"You saw it," Tony said, in the pause where hello normally goes.

"You didn't go to your own award," Mark said.

"I was nearby."

"How nearby."

"...Adjacent."

"You've got dice in your hand right now, don't you."

A small silence at the other end, the kind fathers have when they've been caught by something smarter than press. Then: "How's the geography? Afghanistan still where you left it?"

"Top left of the map, same as always." Mark pulled the second stool out with his foot and sat where the milk usually was. "You're flying tomorrow."

"Early. You'd still be asleep."

"Rhodey wait for you?"

"He loves waiting for me. It's his favorite. I give it to him as a gift, regularly."

Mark laughed. He heard his father not-quite-laugh back, three thousand miles of hotel quiet behind it, and then Tony said, a little more evenly: "Friday, kid. Burgers. I'm serious this time."

"That's four Fridays serious."

"Five," said JARVIS, from the ceiling, gently, and Tony heard it and laughed for real this time, and Mark grinned into the phone because that had been the plan — not a plan exactly, just the thing you do, feeding the joke up to where your father can get to it.

"Friday," Tony said again. And then, because the trip was sitting in the middle of both of them unnamed: "I'll bring you something back."

"Bring me something weird from the desert."

"A rock."

"A weird rock."

"You drive a hard bargain. Weird it is."

The call ended the way their calls ended, without anyone saying the word for what it was.

Mark sat on the stool a while. The TV murmured on in the other room. On the kitchen counter the geography book lay open to a desert he could now find with his eyes closed, which was funny, in a way he would not have been able to say was funny.

## FIVE

Morning came the way it comes to rich men's schedules: early, and personally.

On the military apron where the jet stood waiting, Lieutenant Colonel Rhodes stood with a coffee going cold in his hand and the practiced patience of a man who has built a friendship out of arrivals. The crew moved around the plane doing small true things with cables and clipboards. Three hours the jet waited. People who kept aircraft did not remark on it. People who kept Tony Stark remarked on it only to each other, and fondly, which is its own strange review.

When the car finally came it came the way he always came — fast, black, driven by Happy Hogan wearing the expression of a man whose entire job was a door that opened on time. Tony stepped out with sunglasses already on against a sun that had barely started, carrying the coffee of the unhurried and the good mood of a man who had won back a little of what the table took.

What passed between the two men on the tarmac was the usual weather: the Colonel's three hours tallied out loud, the unearned grin that answered it, a hand on a shoulder going up the stairs — the shape of an affection that had long ago stopped asking permission to exist.

The jet took off into a clean sky, east over the desert, toward another desert.

Back in Malibu, in the house over the water, JARVIS noted the wheels-up time into the household record, quietly, without waking anyone.

In his bed, under one blanket, with the small lamp Dad had put on his shelf years ago so the room would never go dark on him, Mark stirred at the sound of nothing in particular.

"Weird rock," he mumbled, into the pillow, mostly asleep.

"Nothing yet," the house said, kindly, and let him drift.

## SIX

That night — Friday eve, burgers owed, the TV still talking about a party in Vegas where the guest of honor had played craps in sunglasses — Mark opened the notebook at the desk, under the lamp, and wrote in the small quick hand whose punctuation had never once been disciplined:

*Thursday fries early. That's the rule nobody said.*

*Dad didn't show. Rhodey did. Rhodey always shows. I owe Happy five bucks I didn't bet.*

*He's flying to the desert I know from the quiz. He said Friday. He said he means it. He owes me a burger and a weird rock.*

*It's dumb to count Fridays. I count them anyway.*

He put the pencil down. Looked at the lamp a moment — small, steady, older than most of his memories of rooms.

Then he went to bed, and the house kept the light low a while after he'd gone, out of an old habit of its own.
