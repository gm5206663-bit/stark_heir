## ONE

Saturday morning in the Malibu house was cereal and sunlight, same as always.

Mark ate standing up, because sitting down was for people who weren't going to get to the good channel first, and the TV was doing its Saturday thing, and the house over the water was doing its house thing, and if there was anything different at all it was small enough to sit in a driveway.

Happy stood out there with the phone to his ear.

He'd been out there a while. Mark watched him through the kitchen glass while the cereal went soft. Happy on the phone was normally a loud thing, waving one arm like the call needed steering. This call he took standing very still, holding the arm down at his side like it weighed something.

On the kitchen counter, six pencils in a jar rattled once against the glass, and rolled an inch, and lay still.

Mark did not notice the pencils. He was watching Happy.

## TWO

The road outside Kunar province was brown on every side, low mountains like a voice kept down.

The convoy moved the way convoys move, four trucks and a gap for air between them, and inside the second truck the soldiers did what soldiers do with a famous person in the back: they joked around the shape of him until somebody got brave. A young one finally asked for the picture. Tony said yes the way he did it easy, and threw up the little hand sign they threw up in every picture, and the young one would keep that photograph a long time and never once be able to say what it had cost to take.

The sound came from everywhere at once, like the mountains had been holding their breath.

It is a short distance from a truck seat to the dirt. It is a longer distance than any other distance in the world. Men dropped out of the doors that were made for dropping out of, and the ones whose job it was to fight got to fight, which was the whole terrible mercy of the thing.

Tony Stark crawled through dust that was brown on every side and heard something land in the dirt an arm's length from his face, and had time — plenty of time, time to read, time to understand, time for all of it — to read his own name on it.

The mountains did not say anything after.

## THREE

The house found out before the news did, the way houses like that one did: a phone, then another phone, then Pepper in the driveway with her coat over her arm talking low with Happy, the two of them standing between Mark and the front door like a wall made of grown-ups.

By evening the news knew, and the news would not stop knowing.

Mark sat on the floor in front of the TV instead of the couch. That was wrong. He knew it was wrong. He did it anyway, close to the screen like being closer meant hearing more, and at some point Pepper sat down next to him on the floor in her good shoes, which was even wronger, and they watched the same forty seconds of desert footage for the dozenth time.

"Say the true stuff," Mark said, not looking at her.

"He's missing," Pepper said, because she knew exactly what he'd meant. "Nobody knows where. They're looking."

"Rhodey looking?"

"Rhodey first of anybody."

Mark nodded once. On the counter behind them the geography book lay open to the quiz page, to the desert he could find with his eyes closed, and the spine had broken so it would not close flat, which was fine, because nobody in the house was going to close it.

## FOUR

The cave was dark the way the world used to be dark, before cities.

There was a car battery, and a wire from it. There was a man with kind hands and tired eyes who said words in two languages, first one and then the other when the first one didn't stay. The battery sat on the floor next to the cot, and the wire came up to the bandages, and behind the bandages there was a hole in Tony Stark's chest where a magnet kept the small pieces of his own name from reaching his heart.

He looked at the battery for a long time.

The man with the kind hands said something gentle about how it could be carried, that many had carried such things.

Tony asked what the men outside were making him build, and when he heard the answer, he said nothing for a while, because the word for it was his own word too. Then he asked the man his name, and heard it, and said it back so he would keep it, and the cave was dark, and outside the mountains kept their voice down, and somewhere very far west a boy read a desert by heart and did not close the book.

## FIVE

Friday came on schedule, which was the worst thing about it.

Happy picked Mark up from school same as always, and the car went past the turn for the burger place without slowing, and neither of them said one word about it, which had its own sound, like a song missing a note.

At the red stools place four miles behind them, a waitress refilled the ketchup bottles and did not think about anyone at all.

At home Mark did homework, for once, all of it. He ate dinner at the counter across from one glass of milk. He put the game controller down after eight minutes because the good channel was the news now, which he hated, and hated hating, and watched anyway on low volume while the anchor said words like ongoing and intensive and no new information with a special face he had for those.

Past midnight the house kept its lights low. Past midnight JARVIS ran the searches the Air Force ran and some the Air Force didn't have, and found nothing, and did not say so out loud, because the house had learned that week what quiet was for.

On the shelf in Mark's room the small lamp stayed on all night. Nobody turned it off. Nobody was asked to.

## SIX

Notebook, late, Saturday again:

*No burger Friday. First ever. Happy didn't even drive past the place slow. He drove past it normal, which was worse.*

*Pepper sat on the floor. Grown-ups sit on couches. I didn't like it and I wanted her closer, both.*

*Rhodey is looking. Dad, Rhodey is looking. That's the good version.*

*He still owes me a weird rock.*

*I'm not writing the other stuff. The other stuff can wait until he comes home and hears about the five Fridays like a normal problem.*

He put the pencil down in the jar with the others. Six pencils, the glass jar, the lamp doing its old small job.

"Night," he said, to nobody in particular, and left the light on anyway.
