## ONE

Saturday morning in the Malibu house was cereal and sunlight, same as always.

Mark ate standing up, because sitting down was for people who weren't going to get to the good channel first. He had a face the world had never once been casual about — twelve years old and strangers still slowed near him like traffic near something beautiful — and he ate his cereal over the sink with that face set on cartoons, paying the world's attention its usual no-mind.

He had already done his twelve breaths, in and out, sitting straight on the edge of his bed with his eyes closed. He had done them every morning since the sleeping spells when he was little, since the nice doctor with the calm voice taught the counting to a boy who slept like winter. Nobody made him do it anymore. That was how you knew it was his.

Saturday had a shape in that house, and the shape was: garage-tour if Dad was home, couch kingdom if he wasn't, sauce night either way because Happy's sauce didn't care about geopolitics. The surf did its endless patient thing below the windows. The cartoons did theirs above the bowl.

Then the driveway sound.

Happy stood out there with the phone to his ear, and he had been out there a while. Happy on a phone was a loud thing — one arm waving, steering the call like traffic, a man built out of reasonable volume. This call he took very still, the free arm held flat against his own thigh, pressed there, holding himself steady. His chin was doing the small thing it did in photographs of funerals. The morning was warm and Happy's face had gone somewhere with snow in it.

Mark stood at the kitchen glass and read the driveway the way other kids read comics. Nobody waves when it's nothing. Nobody's thumb goes white on a phone when it's traffic school.

"Mark," JARVIS said from the ceiling, in his regular voice, "your cereal is reaching an optimal sogginess window."

"JARVIS." Mark didn't turn from the glass. "Who's dead?"

The pause that came back was one half-second longer than honest.

"No one is dead, Mark."

"You counted to one before you answered. You always count to one when you're deciding what's allowed."

"Am I so obvious to you?"

"You count to two if it's really bad," Mark said. "What was it, one and a half?"

On the counter behind him, six pencils in a glass jar rattled once against each other, rolled an inch, and lay still.

Mark set the bowl down very carefully in the sink, like the day had become a thing you carried with both hands.

## TWO

The road outside Kunar was brown on every side, the mountains standing back like a voice kept low.

The convoy moved the way convoys move — trucks with a gap of air between them, dust following after like something that had been invited. In the second truck the soldiers rode the way soldiers ride near the end of a safe thing: shoulders down, jokes up, equipment riding where equipment rides, and at the far end of the bench sat the famous man in sunglasses, clean as money, on his way to show the desert what winter was going to look like.

They were easy with him the way people get easy after a few days. The two up front argued music with the fluency of men who had argued music on every road in the province — the driver's one driver said the classics were the classics, the other one said the classics were what stations played when the DJs quit caring. The first one said that was a whole thing, and they both looked in the mirror to see if the billionaire was listening, and he was.

"Respect your elders," Tony said mildly from the back. "Also the man is wrong, but respect him anyway. He has the wheel and the gun."

Laughter, quick and grateful, the kind men let out when a hierarchy turns out to be a person. Somebody's mother looked out from a folded photograph passed down the bench. A bet nobody would remember passed the other way and came back settled.

"Sir." The young one, finally, the way a diver finally leaves the board. "Would it be all right — a picture?"

Tony said yes the way he said it everywhere, easy, and leaned in toward the kid's phone. The truck noise made a parenthesis around them. The kid held the phone at arm's length and got his grin ready, and Tony threw up the little two-finger sign he threw up in every photograph anyone had ever taken of him.

"Peace," the kid said, grinning at his own screen. "Love and peace."

He would keep that photograph a long time.

The sound came from everywhere at once, like the mountains were done holding their breath.

The first truck stopped being a truck.

There is no sentence that prepares a person for it, and it doesn't need one: it prepares itself, fast and total, the air going loud the way water goes cold — all at once, through everything. The gap of air between the trucks became the whole world. Doors opened that were made to open. The ones whose job it was to fight got out and fought — the young one with the photograph first into the dust — and the fight was the whole terrible mercy of it, and it did not last long.

Tony Stark got out into brown air that did not care about his name and found the dirt with his cheek and stayed there, the way humble is made. Shouting somewhere. Rifles somewhere else. The truck ticking over its own silence. Something landed in the road an arm's length from his face. Spinning. Settling.

He had time. Plenty of time. Time to read, time to understand, time for all of it.

It said his own name. STARK, neat as a logo on a napkin sketch, stenciled on the shell of a thing made to be sold by the hundreds to the kind of men who had just taken apart his escort in the gap between one heartbeat and the next.

Then the road stood up and took him.

## THREE

Pepper's car came up the driveway at a bad angle for Pepper, and Pepper never parked at a bad angle.

Mark was in the kitchen doorway before the engine stopped. He had the day laid out in front of him now the way he had learned to lay out rooms: Happy's flat hand. JARVIS's half-second. The newspaper nobody had come in to read, face-down on the counter, folded to the wrong section. The coffee pot full past where Happy ever let it get. A house can hold its breath. He had never known that before Saturday, and he would never be able to un-know it.

She came in with her coat still over her arm and stood in the hall light a moment without deciding to be somewhere.

"Coffee," Happy said, moving.

"There's a situation," Pepper said to Mark. Her face was doing the thing where the smile stays on because the smile is a door and behind the door is a room she is not ready to show a kid. He had seen the door before. He had never seen the hallway behind it this crowded.

"There's a situation," she said again, which told him how long she'd practiced the first one in the car. "We're getting the real version before anyone—"

"Then say the real version," Mark said.

Something in her measured him — the way adults did sometimes, checking the height of a boy against the weight of a sentence. Most adults set the sentence down. Pepper picked it up. He would remember that about her for the rest of his life, though he wouldn't have the sentence for it until later: that she had decided he could carry what was true, on the worst morning of all of them.

"There's been an attack," she said. "On the road, after the demonstration. Your father is missing. Nobody knows where yet. Everyone who can look is looking — Colonel Rhodes was in the air before the embassy finished its first sentence."

The kitchen was the same kitchen. The sun kept coming through the same glass at the same angle, like a thing that didn't know. Mark stood very still inside it. Inside his chest his heart did something enormous, and out of the enormous thing he took the only piece he could use.

"Rhodey's good at finding Dad," he said. "He's had practice."

Pepper's mouth did a thing that was almost laughter's cousin. "He has."

"Okay," Mark said, in a voice that had decided to be a grown-up and had gotten the paperwork wrong. "Okay."

He did not cry. He went to his room and closed the door, which was worse, and came back eleven minutes later, because the TV was out here.

By nightfall the news had its forty seconds of desert and would not stop having it. A helicopter shape. A road. A gate with flags he could half-name from the quiz papers on the counter. An anchor with the special face, the one reserved for the wind changed. Mark sat on the floor in front of the screen, closer than allowed, like closer meant more.

At some point Pepper sat down next to him on the floor in her good shoes, which was even wronger, and they watched the same mountains hold their voices down for the dozenth time.

"You should eat," she said, once, without looking at him, which was the only way to say it.

"I did. You should."

"...Fair," she said, and went to the kitchen and made them both toast, and they ate it on the floor in front of the bad mountains, and it was the best meal of the worst day because somebody else was eating it at the same angle of floor.

The geography book lay open on the counter behind them, on the quiz page, on the desert he could find with his eyes closed. Its spine had broken somewhere in the afternoon. It would not close flat now.

Nobody in the house was going to close it.

## FOUR

First there was a voice, in a language he did not know, the same sentence twice. Then the same sentence in English, gently, like being lowered into water.

He came up out of the dark in pieces. Stone room. The smell of iodine over the smell of dust. His own screaming, already finished, somewhere behind him like a dog that had followed him here and been persuaded to stop. A stranger's hands on his shoulders, holding him against the cot the way you'd hold down a frightened animal — patiently, expecting forgiveness later.

A car battery on the floor. A wire from the battery.

The wire came up to his chest. Behind the bandages was a hole, and in the hole a magnet, and the magnet was the only thing between the small pieces of his own munitions and his heart. They took the bandages off and let him see it, because hiding it would not have helped, and the man with the tired eyes and kind hands explained it the way you would explain weather.

We'd like to keep it out of the rain, his manner said, about the shrapnel, about the battery, about the hole in the chest of the most famous engineer alive.

Tony looked at the battery for a long time.

"We met once," the man said, later, over the small clink of instruments being made clean. "Bern. A technical conference. You gave a talk I did not understand, at a party afterward that I understood very well." A small smile, rusted from disuse. "You do not remember."

"I get that a lot," Tony said.

"My name is Yinsen."

Tony said it back once, so that he would keep it. Then he lay back against the cot and stared at a stone ceiling on the far side of the world, and did arithmetic of the heart. How much battery, how long the wire, how many days a man can be a magnet without becoming only a magnet — and let the man with the kind hands ride out his shaking until it was done.

When the men with guns came, they came to say what they wanted, and what they wanted had his name on it too.

They showed him crates. His own crates, his stencils, a warehouse that had migrated. The translator's mouth put the offer on a table: gold, weapons, the cave's whole economy of want, freedom's shape like a mirage behind the guard's eyes. They showed him the missile from the demonstration film, all its clean violence, and asked for one. For one Jericho. Built by the best hands alive.

Tony looked at the pieces laid out in front of him and thought about his hands.

He had built things his whole life by thinking about his hands. These hands that played craps at four in the morning and signed pens and never once, not once in the whole parade of being Tony Stark, felt the weight of what they signed. He looked at the pieces, and felt the magnet shift in his chest like a reminder with good posture, and thought about the hands.

He said no.

They put him under the water trough for the length of a verdict — cold, brown, shouting through it like weather through a crack — and pulled him up by his hair, and asked again. He said no again, wetter. The mountain kept its voice down.

The translator relayed something about days, about what patience costs men with schedules. Then they took him back to the cot, and the wire trailed from his chest like a leash on the sun, and Yinsen held his wrist until the shaking worked its way out, and neither of them said Jericho, and both of them thought it.

## FIVE

On the other side of the planet it was Tuesday, and Tuesday meant school, because school did not know how to stop.

Happy drove him in because Pepper's face that morning was already carrying more freight than a face should, and being kept home would have added to the load. At the gate the crossing guard waved at Mark the way she always waved, big-armed, and then put her hand over her mouth like she'd done something wrong, which was somehow the kindest thing anyone did all morning.

His teachers were kind in the wrong way. Voices turned down around him, like grief was something you shushed. The math teacher told him he could "just observe," and then taught the whole class with a background hope that nobody praised his dad's labor force out loud. The geography teacher did not teach; she put on a documentary about deserts and sat in the back grading papers, and nobody thought that was strange, and he did not either, because the desert on the screen was the desert he was dreaming anyway.

On his desk, when he got back from water-fountain shift minutes, someone had left a milk carton with a folded paper crane against it. His class did that — an arms race of small mercies, a game older than this week. He set the crane on the windowsill where it could oversee. It was a good crane. Engineered. Stable wing geometry, for paper.

In the lunch corridor a ninth-grader with a fence for a mouth said the thing corridors save up. Something about men who make missiles. Something about what goes around. Something about roads, and karma, and a truck, and his supply of material lasting all ten meters of hallway like a net.

The hallway went very quiet and watched, the way hallways do.

Mark stood still. He looked at the boy the way he looked at maps — whole, without hurry. The ninth-grader's jersey was a size too big, with a name on the back too old for the kid wearing it. His knuckles were raw along the right hand. His eyes kept cutting to the stairwell door where somebody he was afraid of usually stood. The hallway's quiet had two kinds, and Mark could read both of them like weather: the pity kind, and the popcorn kind.

"That's your brother's jersey," Mark said.

"What — yeah. So?"

"So you already lost somebody to a road," Mark said, quiet. "You don't wish it on me too. You just can't figure out where else to put it."

The ninth-grader opened his mouth and nothing came out of it. The hallway forgot to breathe.

"It's okay," Mark said. "It's the road's fault. That's the actual enemy. Then everybody else after."

He picked his milk carton back out of the air where he'd set it spinning on one finger — nobody was sure when he'd done that — and walked on. Three lockers down, a pen fell off the top of a locker where it had been balanced since morning, and nobody saw it fall, and even if somebody had seen it fall, they would not have connected it to the boy walking away with his milk and his heart hammering like a loose door, because nobody ever does.

He ate lunch in the car with Happy instead of the cafeteria, which was a violation of everything, and Happy did not report him to anyone. That was how the week went: rules quietly resigning, one after another, wherever he stood.

"You didn't hit him," Happy said, satisfied, unwrapping a sandwich the size of a doorstop.

"I wanted to," Mark said.

"That's my boy."

"I knew exactly where to make it hurt without hitting him, though."

Happy chewed. "That's your father's boy, then."

## SIX

Wednesday night the video came.

It was not on the news. It was worse than the news: a file that traveled between offices, between the cleared and the merely trusted, traveling the way bad things travel when no one can decide whose job it is to stop them, until it reached a dark corner office where Pepper Potts sat alone with the screen the only light in the room.

On the screen: Tony.

Dirt. A shorn look. A bandage. A rifle muzzle near his cropped head, held by hands belonging to a man the camera didn't bother with, in front of a banner of a group whose name the translations were still fighting over. And a paper. Words written for him by hands that did not understand him at all.

He read the words flat and obedient, the way a man reads a ransom note written by the people holding him.

Except.

Pepper's hand came up over her mouth in the dark.

Except that under the obedience his eyes were doing arithmetic. She had watched that man think across ten years of conference tables — the wheel going behind whatever face he was making for the room: boardrooms, courtrooms, senate rooms, parties, bed. She knew the look of it from every angle it had ever taken, and it was there even now, under the dirt and the bandage and the borrowed paper: the wheel going, the wheel going.

Whatever they had between them that had never yet decided its name — that was hers to see it with. The rest of the world would see a hostage. She saw the second thing. She did not thank anyone for it; there was nobody to thank.

She closed the laptop very slowly, as if gentleness could spare the thing inside it. Sat in the dark for a long while. Fixed her face at the bathroom mirror with cold water and the discipline of a dozen impossible mornings, for the drive back up the coast, because there was a boy at the house in Malibu who had not been told there was a video.

That was her mercy to carry. She carried it the whole way home with both hands, like a full bowl.

At eleven, Happy's text came in: house asleep. kid on the couch again. At midnight, her own at long last, she answered: let him sleep where he falls. leave the lamp.

They didn't say goodnight. Nobody in the family of the empty house said goodnight anymore. The words were on strike until further notice.

## SEVEN

In the cave, the work began, because work is what a refusal looks like once you've been pulled out of the water by your hair.

They gave him a room with the equipment of a lesser regime: a welder from somebody's grandfather's war, tools out of somebody else's century, a vise that had opinions. Yinsen moved among the pieces with a field surgeon's patience — which is what he was, had been, in a valley whose name was also a wound: handing things, naming things, translating the mountain and the men in it.

They had told the captors a number of days. The captors had accepted a different number of days. Between those two numbers went all of the rest of it.

Privately, over a scrap heap with his own company's name stenciled on the shrapnel in it — read that again, his own name, stenciled on the shrapnel by hands wearing his factory gloves — Tony held a bit of palladium up to the hanging bulb and looked at it the way men look at doors.

"That doesn't look like a missile part," Yinsen said, in the low voice the cave was for.

"It's not," Tony said. "It's a door."

He did not say door to what. The bulb hummed. Outside, guards counted magazines, and the mountain kept its own counsel, and eleven time zones west a boy sat with an uncloseable book, and both of them began — one with a welder older than his son, one with a pencil and a jar — the long work of waiting with your hands busy so the fear can't get your hands.

## EIGHT

Friday came on schedule, which was the worst thing about it.

Happy picked him up same as always. Three miles of quiet. The radio found a station neither of them listened to and they both let it, because listening would have been a decision. The car went past the turn for the burger place without slowing.

Neither of them said one word about it.

It had its own sound, that not-saying. Like a song missing a note. Four miles behind them, at the red stools, Rhonda refilled the ketchup bottles and wondered briefly where the beautiful kid and his big driver were — and then the fryer beeped, and the thought went where fryer-beep thoughts go.

At home Mark did homework. All of it, for once. He ate dinner across from one glass of milk. He put the game controller down after eight minutes, because the good channel was the news now, which he hated, and hated hating, and watched anyway with the volume low while the anchor said ongoing and intensive and no new information in the special face.

That night he sat on his bed to do the twelve breaths, and lost count at nine, and started over, and lost count at five, and sat in the half-dark with his eyes closed and his chest working like something out in the water. The lamp on his shelf kept its low steady light, older than most of his memories of rooms. On the desk, the pencils in their jar settled a half-inch closer together than he had left them.

On the third try he got to twelve.

He opened his eyes. "JARVIS. Don't tell me any nice versions."

"No nice versions," JARVIS said gently. "Colonel Rhodes's aircraft flew the region again today. There is no new information."

"Okay." Mark got under the covers still in his clothes, which was a rule that had resigned Wednesday. "Leave the lamp."

"As ever," said the house.

## NINE

Notebook, late, Saturday again — a week older than a week should be able to make you — the small quick hand, the punctuation never once disciplined:

*Happy took a call standing like a fencepost. That's how the week started. I knew before anyone said it. I always know before anyone says it. That's not magic. It's looking at what's there.*

*Burger Friday got skipped. First time ever. Happy didn't even drive past the place slow. He drove past it normal. That was worse.*

*People keep looking at my face like it belongs to the news. It's my face. It was mine first.*

*A ninth-grader said the weapon thing in the corridor. I saw the jersey and the knuckles and his eyes going to the stairwell and I knew exactly where his mad really lived, and I said it, and his face went strange like a struck bell. A pen fell off a locker. Probably nothing.*

*Lost my count at nine. Got it back at twelve. The pencils know why. I'm not asking them.*

*Pepper sat on the floor. Grown-ups sit on couches. I didn't like it and I wanted her closer. Both at once. She made toast. The floor is where the week lived, so that's where we ate.*

*Rhodey is looking with planes. Dad — Rhodey is looking with planes. That's the good version.*

*He still owes me a weird rock.*

*The notebook is keeping the light on. Come home and see.*

He put the pencil back in the jar with the others. Six pencils, now standing closer than they used to, in the lamplight, in the quiet house over the water.

"Night," he said, to nobody in particular.

And left the light on anyway.
