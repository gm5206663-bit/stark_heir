## ONE

Saturday morning in the Malibu house was cereal and sunlight, same as always.

Mark ate standing up, because sitting down was for people who weren't going to get to the good channel first. The house over the water did its house thing. If there was anything different, it was small enough to stand in a driveway.

Happy stood out there with the phone to his ear, and he had been out there a while. Happy on a phone was normally a loud thing — one arm waving, steering the call like traffic. This call he took very still, the arm held down at his side like it weighed something.

"Happy?" Mark said, when the door opened. "Who was—"

"Nobody." Happy came in and picked up the newspaper he'd already read, and opened it to the sports page and did not turn it. "Eat your cereal."

"You're holding it upside down."

Happy looked at the paper. Turned it over. Kept not reading it.

On the kitchen counter, six pencils in a glass jar rattled once against each other, and rolled an inch, and lay still.

Mark did not notice the pencils. He was watching the man at the counter not read the newspaper, and some part of him, twelve years old and doing its job, filed it away under things to worry about later, because Saturday was for cartoons.

## TWO

The road outside Kunar was brown on every side, the mountains standing back like a voice kept low.

The convoy moved the way convoys move — trucks with a gap of air between them, dust following after like something that had been invited. In the second truck the soldiers rode the way soldiers ride near the end of a safe thing, shoulders down, jokes up, and at the far end of the bench sat the famous man in sunglasses, clean as money, on his way to show the desert what winter was going to look like.

They were easy with him the way people get easy after a few days — the front-seat kids arguing music, somebody's mother in a folded photograph, a bet about something nobody would remember.

"Sir." The young one, finally, the way a diver finally leaves the board. "Can I— would it be all right, a picture?"

Tony said yes the way he said it everywhere, easy, and leaned in toward the kid's phone, and threw up the little two-finger sign he threw up in every photograph of him anyone had ever taken.

"Peace," the kid said, grinning at his own screen. "Love and peace."

He would keep that photograph a long time.

The sound came from everywhere at once, like the mountains had been holding their breath and were done with it.

The first truck stopped being a truck. The gap of air between the trucks became the whole world. Drivers did what drivers are trained to do, and it was not enough, and doors opened that were made to open, and the ones whose job it was to fight got out and fought — the young one with the photograph first into the dust, which was the whole terrible mercy of it: there was a fight, and they were in it.

Tony Stark got out into brown air that did not care about his name and found the dirt with his cheek and stayed there, the way humble is made. Something landed in the road an arm's length from his face, spinning, settling.

He had time. Plenty of time. Time to read, time to understand, time for all of it.

It said his own name. It said STARK on the shell of it, neat as a logo on a napkin sketch, made to be sold in the hundreds to the kind of men who had just killed his escort in the gap between one heartbeat and the next.

Then the road stood up and took him.

## THREE

The house knew before the news did, the way houses like that one knew: a phone, then another phone, then Pepper's car in the driveway at a bad angle for Pepper, and the two of them — Pepper and Happy — standing between Mark and the front door like a wall made of grown-ups.

"There's a situation," Happy said. "We're getting the real version before—"

"Then say the real version," Mark said.

They told him. Missing. An attack on the road after the demonstration. Nobody knew. Everybody who could look was looking, Rhodey first of anybody, Rhodey with aircraft over the mountains before the embassy had finished its first sentence.

Mark stood with his backpack on one shoulder, the broken strap, Saturday nowhere in sight.

"Okay," he said, in a voice that had decided to be a grown-up and had gotten the paperwork wrong. "Okay."

He did not cry. He went to his room and closed the door, which was worse, and opened it again eleven minutes later, because the TV was out here.

By night the news had its forty seconds of desert footage and would not stop having it. Mark sat on the floor in front of the screen, closer than allowed, like being closer meant hearing more. At some point Pepper sat down on the floor next to him in her good shoes, which was even wronger, and they watched the same mountains hold their voices down for the dozenth time.

"Say the true stuff," Mark said, not looking at her.

"He's missing," Pepper said, because she knew exactly what he'd meant. "Nobody knows where. They're looking."

"Rhodey looking."

"Rhodey first of anybody."

Mark nodded once. Behind them, on the counter, the geography book lay open to the quiz page, to the desert he could find with his eyes closed. Its spine had broken somewhere in the afternoon. It would not close flat now.

Nobody in the house was going to close it.

## FOUR

First there was a voice, in a language he did not know, the same sentence twice. Then the same sentence in English, gently, like being lowered into water.

He came up out of the dark in pieces. Stone room. His own screaming, already finished, somebody else's hands on his shoulders holding him against the cot like a thing that needed holding down. A car battery on the floor. A wire from the battery.

The wire came up to his chest. Behind the bandages there was a hole, and in the hole a magnet, and the magnet was the only thing between the small pieces of his own munitions and his heart, and the man with the tired eyes and kind hands explained this to him the way you'd explain weather: we'd like to keep it out of the rain.

Tony looked at the battery for a long time.

"We met once," the man said. "Bern. A conference. You gave a talk I did not understand, at a party I did understand." A small smile, rusted from disuse. "You do not remember."

"I get that a lot."

"My name is Yinsen."

Tony said it back, once, so that he would keep it. Then he lay back on the cot and stared at the stone ceiling of a cave in a mountain on the far side of the world, and did arithmetic of the heart, and let the man with the kind hands hold his shaking until it was done. Outside, the mountain said nothing. The men with the guns had not come yet to say what they wanted.

When they came, they came to say what they wanted, and what they wanted had his name on it too.

They showed him crates. His own crates, his own stencils, stockpiled like a warehouse had migrated. They showed him the missile from the demonstration film, all its clean violence, and through a translator's mouth they put a kingdom on the table: gold and women and guns and the cave's whole economy of want, for one Jericho, built by the best hands alive.

Tony looked at the pieces laid out in front of him and thought about his hands.

He said no.

They put him under the water trough for the length of a verdict, and pulled him up by the hair, and asked again.

## FIVE

On the other side of the planet it was Tuesday, and Tuesday meant school, because school did not know how to stop.

Mark went, because Pepper's face that morning was already carrying more than a face should, and being sent back home would have added to the freight. He sat in classes with the TV-famous emptiness in the chair next to his name, and the teachers were kind in the wrong way — voices turned down around him, like grief was something you shushed.

In the corridor at lunch a ninth-grader with a fence for a mouth said the thing corridors save up: something about men who make missiles, and what goes around, and roads.

Mark stood still. The hallway watched the way hallways watch.

"Say it again," Mark said, "and tell me which part of it helps."

He walked on. His heart was doing something in his chest that nobody could see, and three lockers down a pen fell off the top of a locker where it had been balanced all morning, and nobody could see that either, and it did not matter, because nobody in that hallway was looking at pens.

He ate lunch with Happy in the car instead of the cafeteria — a violation of everything — and Happy did not report him to anyone, and that was how the week went: rules quietly resigning, one after another, wherever he stood.

## SIX

Wednesday night the video came.

It was not on the news. It was worse than the news: a file that traveled between offices, between people with clearances and people without, until it reached the dark corner office where Pepper Potts sat alone with the screen the only light in the room.

On the screen: Tony. Dirt, a shorn look, a bandage. A man behind him with a rifle muzzle near his cropped head, and words being read off a paper that had been written for him.

He read the words. He read them the way a man reads a ransom note written by the people holding him, flat and obedient, except — Pepper's hand came up over her mouth in the dark — except that somewhere under the obedience his eyes were doing arithmetic. She had been watching that man think for years across conference tables. She knew what it looked like even through a hostage video, even with a gun to his skull: the wheel going, the wheel going.

She closed the laptop very slowly, like a thing that could be spared by gentleness, and sat in the dark, and did not know that at home in Malibu a boy had not been told there was a video, because there are mercies, and that was hers: to carry the picture so he wouldn't have to.

## SEVEN

In the cave, the work began, because work is what a refusal looks like once you've been pulled out of the water by your hair.

They gave him a room with the equipment of a lesser regime — a welder, tools out of somebody else's century — and Yinsen moved among the pieces with the patience of a field surgeon, which is what he was, handing things, naming things, translating the mountains. They had told the captors a number of days. Privately, over a scrap heap with his own company's name stenciled on the shrapnel in it, Tony held a palladium ring up to the hanging bulb and looked at it the way men look at doors.

"That doesn't look like a missile part," Yinsen said, in the low voice the cave was for.

"It's not," Tony said. "It's a door."

He did not say door to what. The bulb hummed. Outside, the guards counted their magazines, and the mountain kept its own counsel, and eleven time zones west a boy sat with an uncloseable book.

## EIGHT

Friday came on schedule, which was the worst thing about it.

Happy picked Mark up from school same as always. The car went past the turn for the burger place without slowing, and neither of them said one word about it — which had its own sound, like a song missing a note.

At the red stools, four miles behind them, a waitress refilled the ketchup bottles and did not think about anyone at all.

At home Mark did homework — all of it, for once. He ate dinner at the counter across from one glass of milk. He put the game controller down after eight minutes, because the good channel was the news now, which he hated, and hated hating, and watched anyway on low volume while the anchor said ongoing and intensive and no new information in the special face.

Past midnight the house kept its lights low. Past midnight JARVIS ran the searches the Air Force ran, and several the Air Force did not have, and found nothing, and said nothing, because that week the house had learned what quiet was for.

On the shelf in Mark's room the small lamp stayed on all night. Nobody turned it off. Nobody was asked to.

## NINE

Notebook, late, Saturday again — the small quick hand, the punctuation never once disciplined:

*Burger Friday got skipped. First time ever. Happy didn't even drive past the place slow. He drove past it normal. That was worse.*

*Pepper sat on the floor. Grown-ups sit on couches. I didn't like it and I wanted her closer. Both at once.*

*School said a thing about weapons and roads in the corridor. I didn't fix it. I walked away and a pen fell off a locker. Probably nothing.*

*Rhodey is looking with planes. Dad, Rhodey is looking with planes. That's the good version.*

*He still owes me a weird rock.*

*I'm not writing the other stuff. The other stuff can wait until he comes home, and then it goes back to being a normal problem, like the Fridays. Come home. The notebook is keeping the light on.*

He put the pencil back in the jar with the others. Six pencils. One lamp. A book on a counter, open, that nobody would close.

"Night," he said, to nobody in particular.

And left the light on anyway.
