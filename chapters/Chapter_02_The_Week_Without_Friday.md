## ONE

Saturday morning in the Malibu house was cereal and sunlight, same as always.

Mark ate standing up, because sitting down was for people who weren't going to get to the good channel first. He had a face the world had never once been casual about — twelve years old and strangers still slowed near him like traffic near something beautiful — and he ate his cereal over the sink with that face set on cartoons, paying the world's attention its usual no-mind.

He had already done his twelve breaths, in and out, sitting straight on his bed with his eyes closed. He had done them every morning since the sleeping spells when he was little, since the nice doctor with the calm voice had taught the counting to a boy who slept like winter. Nobody made him do it anymore. That was how you knew it was his.

Then the driveway sound.

Happy stood out there with the phone to his ear, and he had been out there a while. Happy on a phone was a loud thing — one arm waving, steering the call like traffic. This call he took very still, the arm held down at his side like it weighed something.

Mark stood at the kitchen glass and read the driveway the way other kids read comics. Happy's free hand had gone flat against his own thigh, pressed there, holding himself steady. His chin was doing the small thing it did at funerals in photographs. The morning was warm and Happy's face had gone somewhere with snow in it.

"Mark," JARVIS said from the ceiling, in his regular voice, "your cereal is reaching an optimal sogginess window."

"JARVIS." Mark didn't turn from the glass. "Who's dead?"

The pause that came back was one half-second longer than honest.

"No one is dead, Mark."

On the counter behind him, six pencils in a glass jar rattled once against each other, rolled an inch, and lay still.

Mark set the bowl down very carefully in the sink, like the day had become a thing you carried with both hands.

## TWO

The road outside Kunar was brown on every side, the mountains standing back like a voice kept low.

The convoy moved the way convoys move — trucks with a gap of air between them, dust following after like something that had been invited. In the second truck the soldiers rode the way soldiers ride near the end of a safe thing, shoulders down, jokes up, and at the far end of the bench sat the famous man in sunglasses, clean as money, on his way to show the desert what winter was going to look like.

They were easy with him the way people get easy after a few days. The front-seat kids argued music. Somebody's mother looked out from a folded photograph. A bet nobody would remember passed down the bench and came back settled.

"Sir." The young one, finally, the way a diver finally leaves the board. "Would it be all right — a picture?"

Tony said yes the way he said it everywhere, easy, and leaned in toward the kid's phone, and threw up the little two-finger sign he threw up in every photograph anyone had ever taken of him.

"Peace," the kid said, grinning at his own screen. "Love and peace."

He would keep that photograph a long time.

The sound came from everywhere at once, like the mountains were done holding their breath.

The first truck stopped being a truck. The gap of air became the whole world. Doors opened that were made to open, and the ones whose job it was to fight got out and fought — the young one with the photograph first into the dust, which was the whole terrible mercy of it: there was a fight, and they were in it, and it did not last long.

Tony Stark got out into brown air that did not care about his name and found the dirt with his cheek and stayed there, the way humble is made. Something landed in the road an arm's length from his face. Spinning. Settling.

He had time. Plenty of time. Time to read, time to understand, time for all of it.

It said his own name. It said STARK on the shell casing, neat as a logo on a napkin sketch, made to be sold by the hundreds to the kind of men who had just taken apart his escort in the gap between one heartbeat and the next.

Then the road stood up and took him.

## THREE

Pepper's car came up the driveway at a bad angle for Pepper, and Pepper never parked at a bad angle.

Mark was in the kitchen doorway before the engine stopped. He had the day laid out in front of him now the way he had learned to lay out rooms: Happy's flat hand, JARVIS's half-second, the newspaper nobody had come in to read sitting face-down on the counter with the dealer's tab upside on top, folded to the obituaries page.

She came in with her coat still over her arm, and she stood in the hall light a moment without deciding to be somewhere.

"Coffee," Happy said, moving.

"There's a situation," Pepper said to Mark, and her face was doing the thing where the smile stays on because the smile is a door and behind the door is a room she is not ready to show a kid. "We're getting the real version before anyone—"

"Then say the real version," Mark said.

Something in her eyes measured him — the way adults did sometimes, checking the height of a boy against the weight of a sentence. Most adults set it down. Pepper picked it up.

"There's been an attack," she said. "On the road, after the demonstration. Your father is missing. Nobody knows where yet. Everyone who can look is looking — Colonel Rhodes was in the air before the embassy finished its first sentence."

The kitchen was the same kitchen. The sun kept coming through the same glass at the same angle. Mark stood very still inside it, and inside his chest his heart did something enormous, and out of the enormous thing he took the only piece he could use.

"Rhodey's good at finding Dad," he said. "He's had practice."

Pepper's mouth did a thing that was almost laughter's cousin. "He has."

"Okay," Mark said, in a voice that had decided to be a grown-up and had gotten the paperwork wrong. "Okay."

He did not cry. He went to his room and closed the door, which was worse, and came back eleven minutes later, because the TV was out here.

By nightfall the news had its forty seconds of desert and would not stop having it. Mark sat on the floor in front of the screen, closer than allowed, like closer meant more. Pepper sat down next to him on the floor in her good shoes, which was even wronger, and they watched the same mountains hold their voices down for the dozenth time.

The geography book lay open on the counter behind them, on the quiz page, on the desert he could find with his eyes closed. Its spine had broken somewhere in the afternoon. It would not close flat now. Nobody in the house was going to close it.

## FOUR

First there was a voice, in a language he did not know, the same sentence twice. Then the same sentence in English, gently, like being lowered into water.

He came up out of the dark in pieces. Stone room. His own screaming, already finished. A stranger's hands on his shoulders, holding him against the cot like a thing that needed holding down. A car battery on the floor. A wire from the battery.

The wire came up to his chest. Behind the bandages was a hole, and in the hole a magnet, and the magnet was the only thing between the small pieces of his own munitions and his heart. The man with the tired eyes and kind hands explained it the way you would explain weather: we'd like to keep it out of the rain.

Tony looked at the battery for a long time.

"We met once," the man said. "Bern. A conference. You gave a talk I did not understand, at a party I understood very well." A small smile, rusted from disuse. "You do not remember."

"I get that a lot."

"My name is Yinsen."

Tony said it back, once, so that he would keep it. Then he lay back against the cot and stared at a stone ceiling on the far side of the world, and did arithmetic of the heart, and let the man with the kind hands ride out his shaking until it was done.

When the men with guns came, they came to say what they wanted, and what they wanted had his name on it too.

They showed him crates — his own crates, his stencils, a warehouse that had migrated. They showed him the missile from the demonstration film, all its clean violence, and through a translator's mouth they put a kingdom on the table: gold, weapons, the cave's whole economy of want, for one Jericho from the best hands alive.

Tony looked at the pieces laid out in front of him and thought about his hands.

He said no.

They put him under the water trough for the length of a verdict, and pulled him up by his hair, and asked again, and the mountain kept its voice down.

## FIVE

On the other side of the planet it was Tuesday, and Tuesday meant school, because school did not know how to stop.

Mark went in because Pepper's face that morning was already carrying more freight than a face should, and being kept home would have added to the load. He walked his corridor with that face of his over the collar of a gray hoodie, and the corridor did what corridors had done around him since kindergarten — it parted, and it looked, and today it looked longer than usual, because today his face was on the news.

His teachers were kind in the wrong way: voices turned down around him, like grief was something you shushed. His friends orbited carefully. Someone had left a milk carton on his desk with a folded paper crane against it, which was a thing his class did; he set the crane on the windowsill where it could oversee.

In the lunch corridor a ninth-grader with a fence for a mouth said the thing corridors save up. Something about men who make missiles. Something about what goes around. Something about roads.

The hallway went very quiet and watched, the way hallways do.

Mark stood still. He looked at the boy the way he looked at maps — whole, without hurry. The ninth-grader's jersey was a size too big and had a name on the back too old for the kid wearing it. His knuckles were raw. His eyes kept cutting to the stairwell door, where someone he was afraid of usually stood.

"That's your brother's jersey," Mark said.

"What — yeah. So?"

"So you already lost somebody to a road," Mark said, quiet. "You don't wish it on me too. You just can't figure out where else to put it."

The ninth-grader opened his mouth and nothing came out of it. The hallway forgot to breathe. Mark picked his milk carton back out of the air where he'd set it spinning on one finger — nobody was sure when he'd done that — and walked on, and behind him a pen fell off the top of a locker where it had been balanced since morning, and nobody saw it fall.

He ate lunch in the car with Happy instead of the cafeteria, which was a violation of everything, and Happy did not report him to anyone. That was how the week went: rules quietly resigning, one after another, wherever he stood.

## SIX

Wednesday night the video came.

It was not on the news. It was worse than the news: a file that traveled between offices, between the cleared and the merely trusted, until it reached a dark corner office where Pepper Potts sat alone with the screen the only light in the room.

On the screen: Tony. Dirt. A shorn look, a bandage. A rifle muzzle near his cropped head, and a paper of words written for him by hands that did not understand him at all.

He read the words flat and obedient, the way a man reads a ransom note written by the people holding him — except.

Pepper's hand came up over her mouth in the dark.

Except that under the obedience his eyes were doing arithmetic. She had watched that man think across ten years of conference tables. She knew the look of it even through a hostage video, even with a gun at his skull: the wheel going, the wheel going.

She closed the laptop very slowly, as if gentleness could spare the thing inside it, and sat in the dark for a long while. There was a boy at home in Malibu who had not been told there was a video. That was her mercy to carry, and she carried it.

## SEVEN

In the cave the work began, because work is what a refusal looks like once you've been pulled out of the water by your hair.

They gave him a room with the equipment of a lesser regime — a welder, tools out of somebody else's century. Yinsen moved among the pieces with a field surgeon's patience, which is what he was: handing things, naming things, translating the mountain. They had told the captors a number of days.

Privately, over a scrap heap with his own company's name stenciled on the shrapnel in it, Tony held a bit of palladium up to the hanging bulb and looked at it the way men look at doors.

"That doesn't look like a missile part," Yinsen said, in the low voice the cave was for.

"It's not," Tony said. "It's a door."

He did not say door to what. The bulb hummed. Outside, guards counted magazines, and the mountain kept its own counsel, and eleven time zones west a boy sat with an uncloseable book.

## EIGHT

Friday came on schedule, which was the worst thing about it.

Happy picked him up same as always. The car went past the burger-place turn without slowing, and neither of them said one word about it — which had its own sound, like a song missing a note.

At the red stools, four miles behind them, a waitress refilled ketchup bottles and wondered, briefly, where the beautiful kid and his big driver were, and then the fryer beeped and the thought went with it.

At home Mark did homework — all of it, for once. He ate dinner across from one glass of milk. He put the game controller down after eight minutes, because the good channel was the news now, which he hated, and hated hating, and watched anyway with the volume low while the anchor said ongoing and intensive and no new information in the special face.

That night he sat on his bed to do the twelve breaths, and lost count at nine, and started over, and lost count at five, and sat in the half-dark with his eyes closed and his chest working like something out in the water, and the lamp on his shelf kept its low steady light, older than most of his memories of rooms.

On the third try he got to twelve. On the desk, the pencils in their jar settled a half-inch closer together than he had left them.

He opened his eyes. "JARVIS. Don't tell me any nice versions."

"No nice versions," JARVIS said gently. "Colonel Rhodes's aircraft flew the region again today. There is no new information."

"Okay." Mark got under the covers, still in his day clothes, which was a rule that had resigned Tuesday. "Leave the lamp."

"As ever."

## NINE

Notebook, late, Saturday again — the small quick hand, the punctuation never once disciplined:

*Happy took a call standing like a fencepost. That's how the week started. I knew before anyone said it. I always know before anyone says it. That's not magic, it's just looking.*

*Burger Friday got skipped. First ever. Happy didn't even drive past the place slow. He drove past it normal. That was worse.*

*People keep looking at my face like it belongs to the news. It's my face. It was mine first.*

*A ninth-grader said the weapon thing in the corridor. I saw his knuckles and the jersey and I knew exactly where his mad really lived. I told him so. His face went strange like a struck bell. A pen fell off a locker. Probably nothing.*

*Lost my count at nine. Got it back at twelve. The pencils know why. I'm not asking them.*

*Pepper sat on the floor. Grown-ups sit on couches. I didn't like it and I wanted her closer. Both at once.*

*Rhodey is looking with planes. Dad — Rhodey is looking with planes. That's the good version.*

*He still owes me a weird rock.*

*The notebook is keeping the light on. Come home and see.*

He put the pencil back in the jar with the others. Six pencils, now standing closer than they used to, in the lamplight, in the quiet house over the water.

"Night," he said, to nobody in particular.

And left the light on anyway.
