*PANEL ONE — THE AIRFIELD, MORNING*

The jet came down out of the marine layer like something being let back into the world a piece at a time, and the first thing off the top of the stairs was the left arm in the sling, and the second thing was all the rest of him.

Tony Stark stood against the light in borrowed fatigues with his good hand raised to shade his eyes, thinner in the face and older around the eyes than any photograph had the right to predict. Pepper Potts, at the bottom of the stairs with Happy a half-step behind her shoulder, forgot all twelve of the things she had planned to say.

"You're crying," Tony said, coming down carefully, step by step, like each stair was a negotiation. "For me?"

"Tears of joy," Pepper said — composure holding one second too long, then breaking on the pivot anyway. "I hate job hunting. Vacation's over."

Happy took the bag off Tony's good shoulder before anyone asked him to and held the door of the car open with the smoothness of a man performing ordinariness as hard as he could. "Welcome home, Mr. Stark," Happy said, and his voice came out rougher than professional, and he didn't fix it.

"Missed your driving, Hogan."

"Missed needing to drive, sir."

Tony looked between the two of them, then past them, at the road unspooling toward the coast under a bright American noon, and then back. The question arrived pre-assembled, first out of the hangar of him, ahead of hospital, ahead of the short list he'd been building in his head since the desert let go of him.

"My kid—" Tony said.

"Home," Pepper said, and the word had been ready for a hundred repetitions. "Waiting. He waited, Tony. The whole time."

Tony's hand settled on the open car door and didn't settle. Air moved through him somewhere that wasn't the lungs' job. "Yeah," he said. "Well. First—"

"We're taking you to the hospital," Pepper said.

"Huh-uh. No." Tony lowered himself into the seat with the cautious extravagance of an old machine in a young decade, and the good hand came up making traffic: *drive.* "No hospitals. Two things in this life are non-negotiable today. One is an American cheeseburger — I want it so much I'm prepared to say grace to it. And the press conference, because that's supposed to happen when? — next week, because Obie's a coward — which means now, today, before I lose my nerve." He settled back. "Car's good. Car drives past the cliff, right, Happy? Same road. Nobody has to lie to it."

"It drives past," Happy said gruffly, and that was that.

*PANEL TWO — THE COAST ROAD, IN THE CAR*

The drive took the coast because the drive always took the coast, and Tony watched the water scroll by in sheets of sun and grey, like film run at the wrong honest speed.

"They gave you the sling," Pepper said, light, careful, from the jump seat.

"They gave me the whole outfit. The sling, the army green, the bedside manner of modern military medicine." The joke walked in tired and lay down. His eyes didn't come far in from the water. "Hey. Pep. They had a picture of my kid on the wall. Via the wall. I looked at that wall until they took the sling off. Thought about him the whole—" The water filled it. The car filled it. The home-quiet filled it. "Thought about the rock."

"The rock," Pepper said.

"He asked me for a rock. An ugly one. His exact—" Tony raised the good hand, palm up, producing a brief. "'The ugliest rock in the province. I want the one that offends everyone.'" The smile wandered real. "I was going to pick it on the way home. Never got the chance." A manageable silence. "I'm going to tell him I forgot. Watch his face. It's the first legal thing I want."

The cheeseburger went in two bites and a long silence that was nearly a prayer, and the car kept the coast.

*PANEL THREE — STARK INDUSTRIES, THE PRESS CONFERENCE, SAME AFTERNOON*

They had the lobby filling with press before Legal finished objecting to the concept.

Reporters came the way weather comes off the ocean — rolling, layered, impossible to refuse — cameras shouldered, mics bristling, producers speaking in murmurs that got louder in inverse proportion to how little anyone trusted the setup. The podium was regulation: flags, the company logo, a glass of water no one would drink. And the room changed inventory when the target walked in still wearing army green and a sling and sat down *on the floor* at the edge of the stage with a paper-wrapped cheeseburger in his good hand.

Tony Stark looked up at two hundred stunned camera operators like a man at a picnic.

"Sit down," Tony said pleasantly. "Wherever you want. This is informal. There's chairs, there's floor, honestly the floor's fine, I'm on the floor, I'm not a snob about it."

Nobody moved at first, because nobody in that entire industry had a file for this. Then a wire-service kid in the second row sat down on the carpet cross-legged, and six more did it behind him, and the cameras came down and found their angles, and Tony Stark ate about a third of a cheeseburger in front of the assembled power of the American press in comfortable silence.

In the back, Christine Everhart didn't sit down. She kept her recorder up and her face in the shape of a woman actually cataloging, for once, with no idea yet what she was holding.

"Obie—" Tony gestured with the burger, toward the side of the stage where Obadiah Stane stood in a state of visible internal recalculation, "—Obie tried to cancel this. On account of me being tired. I told him I had a nap on the plane." Chewing. "Colonel Rhodes has seen me tired. This is nothing. Proceed while I'm this tired and we all get honesty. Everybody clear on the rules?"

Somebody laughed. Then more people did.

Tony took his time — one more bite — and then set the burger aside on the wax paper with the ceremony you'd give a chess clock sealing a move, and got to his feet with a wince he did not let anybody make into a headline, and stood at the podium in front of his own logo and his own flags.

"I came back from a demonstration in Kunar," he said, and the room went very quiet. "And I came back changed. I saw young Americans killed by what I'd built to protect them. I saw—" a pause, the floor of the room holding its breath, "—that I'd become part of a system that's comfortable with zero accountability. For the profit. For the quarter. For the—" his good hand cut the air, wearily, a man finished with abstractions, "for everything. And I remember thinking: if this is my company — if it's my *name* on the side of it — maybe I personally should have more to offer this world than things that blow up."

The walls were already starting to move — phones lifting, texts launching — and the room's own temperature hadn't caught on yet.

"So effective immediately," Tony Stark said, "I'm shutting down the weapons manufacturing division of Stark International. Effective immediately. I'll be finding us a new lane, and it'll be a big one, and it'll have a conscience attached. That's the statement."

The lobby went up like a struck match.

It was not shouting; it was weather. Every throat going through the same register of disbelief — *Tony! Tony! Sir! — is this permanent? what about shareholders? Mr. Stark, Mr. Stark—* the questions folding over each other at amplifier volume while Tony stood at the podium with his good hand on the glass of water he didn't want, watching the storm he had personally assembled, the face under the army green as thin and tired and perfectly steady as anyone had ever seen it.

In the crush, Everhart was the last voice to rise clearly above the noise: "Mr. Stark — is this a withdrawal from Kunar, or a confession about Kunar?"

Tony looked at her for a full half-beat, and then the famous face did the thing it did for strangers: a smile like a wall installed at perfect angles, sealed behind the eyes.

"Both," he said, evenly. "Next question."

Obadiah Stane was already halfway onto the stage — beard catching the lights, palms spread, eye contact with the camera lights going up like it was already regular-season finals. His voice found the register of the larger faith: avuncular, commanding, warm.

"Everybody, everybody—" Lifting the hands; the room settled by force of mass. "We appreciate you being here. What you just heard is a man who came home from the desert and sat down on a floor in front of you all to eat a cheeseburger, which is either therapy or wisdom, and I'm not qualified to say which." Laughter, nervous, the kind that comes easily to people who have just been given an undertow to hold. "Tony Stark is my partner, my friend, and the most important man this company has — and this company is going to keep being this company, and we're going to talk about new directions together with the board like the board is in the room, because some of them actually are." A beat, the smile deep and middle-weathered. "Mind the exits. Tony is not closing down the American dynasty. We're merely—" Obadiah's eyes flicked to Tony, affection and arithmetic passing visibly across each other "—deciding what the dynasty does next. As chess goes, this is one of the good ones."

More questions, more flashes — peppered back and forth — and Obadiah fielded them with the full authority of twenty years of answering for Tony's edges. Tony watched him from the podium hand's reach, listening, hand on the water, not drinking.

The conference broke up like weather.

*MALIBU — THE WORKSHOP, SAME AFTERNOON (cross-panel)*

Mark watched it on the workshop screen with JARVIS quiet above him like a held guttering note, and Happy home by then, and Pepper's car coming up the road with Dad in it — he knew the engine, the coast road order of it, a recognition older than any of the rest — and the screen showed Dad at the podium in army green, saying words that were already changing the company, and Mark's hands were still among the solder trash, perfectly still, the way his hands went when the world's record started to happen without him.

"Obadiah's covering," JARVIS said quietly, watching the reaction shots.

"Obadiah's always covering," Mark said. His eyes were not on Obadiah. His eyes were on the hands around his father's face — the wince, the tired, the kore-line the photos never carried. "Dad changed something at the podium. I don't know what yet. It was there before the desert in him too, but it got bigger."

Happy, at the bench, did not look up. He did not need to. They'd both seen the wince. Some things a driver and a son are in the same profession about.

"He'll want food," Mark said. "Conferences make him eat like it's a hobby. Put both freezers on something."

*PANEL FOUR — MALIBU, THE HOMECOMING, EVENING*

The house that had held its breath for three months finally, at last, exhaled — properly, noisily, down the windowsills and into the floorboards — when the Suburban came up the driveway in the level light.

Mark was on the door lintel before Happy cut the engine.

The door opened. Dad got out. Thinner. Browner. The sling in army green, the borrowed years of him worn deep in every pocket, the face cut down into planes Mark didn't remember. Behind him Pepper in airport armor, the water laid bright below the cliff, and the whole picture stood in hard noon light like a live frame of the word *home* — and all of that was *outside* — Mark registered it at remainder's distance, the whole catalogue frozen mid-entry — because three months of lights-off had issued standing orders without asking him, and somewhere under the surface of the boy he'd been running all summer, something said *get to him.*

He didn't run. He walked, twelve years old and not fooling anyone in this family, down the driveway in bare feet. Dad saw him come, and the famous face did the thing it only did for family — the rear wall of expression gone entirely off its hinges, everything welcome in — and Mark stepped in close and put his arms around his father's good side, low-to-high, twelve years' hold-all.

"Careful, there's — the ribs on that side are full of editorial comments," Tony said, and turned the good shoulder in anyway, folding him in under every possible contribution under the circumstances, chin on hair that carried eleven time zones of darker blond. "Hey, Howard. Hey. You smell like solder. It's the best smell I've—" One clean catch of breath, just the one. "You got tall while I was out. Is that legal?"

"I have a note," Mark said, and got it out too fast for the note to mean anything, and felt his father's *warm* arrive like dawn in installments.

It came in layers, because the sensing had grown new ways of holding things all summer — bandwidth enough for the pressure, texture enough for the differences within it. Warm first: the old register, love of family in the bone, familiar as his own heartbeat. Grief second: canyon-deep, fresh-bandaged, alive every second. Third, under everything: a guard-up stillness Mark didn't know, a carrying of the whole desert locked one inch behind the ribs and never indicated — and, moving through all of it, *thankful-fresh*: gratitude so large it might break into pieces and never be expected to name any of them.

And no wrongness anywhere. Nothing leaning. Nothing shadowed. The ache from the lamp months, the wrongness that had leaned its way across an ocean toward the southeast and stayed stubbornly unproven — the wrongness no longer knew what had become of itself, and it didn't matter, and it was finished.

Obadiah's hug could stop an inch under the cheek. Pepper's arms held steady through two layers of calibration. Happy's shoulder carried worry the size of a suitcase. But Dad's hug went all the way down, down, down, soundings the whole time, and the new reach took the soundings and filed all of them without argument as *alive.* No certificates needed issuing. This wasn't evidence. This was just Dad.

"You forgot the rock," Mark said, wet-sounding into the shirt, steering the whole afternoon toward comedic theater on purpose.

Tony pulled back enough to assemble his face. "*A* rock? In nature, sure, sometimes. Forgetting—"

"The ugly rock. The worst in the Hindu Kush. You were putting it in the briefing, I have notes."

Tony looked at the sky behind him — genuinely theatrical — the eyes wet and the mouth webbed with privilege, and Mark stood there inhaling whole-planet air for the first time in months and watching the performance the way you watch good theater that is also, simultaneously, a wartime creation.

"Here's the thing about beautiful plans," Tony said. "The way home was crowded. Rocks weren't on the ticket. I—" The performance slid, the man came through, the face going hard-wired against everything three months had kept alive below the surface. "I thought about it. Not the rock. The asking. Some kid who already knew where the grid went before the engineers did. I thought about the kid asking. Howard. That's the whole—" He didn't finish it. He put the boy back into the shoulder and held him like something that wasn't hastily assembled around injury. "Bring me a rock another time and I'll tell you a joke about it. Deal?"

"Deal," Mark said, and held on, taller than Dad remembered him, and Dad held them both up like the sky had agreed to one more season of digging in.

Outside them both, Pepper stood with her hands at her mouth in a way from which nothing quiet was defensible ever after, and Happy stood beside her with a driver's passive look that he wore when the losses got buried in the earnings column, and the ocean rolled itself through ordinary reports below the cliff, and nobody moved until it was right, and the afternoon grew quiet — the first real quiet of the season.

"It's okay, Dad," Mark said. "I have notes. Later you'll have to read them. Not all of them, some. And the sling's ugly. I said it first."

His dad laughed through what was actually crying, discovered both circumstances at once, and filed the joke somewhere he'd be able to find it again. *The kid can see the whole of me now. Teach him separate ledgers later.*

"Come inside," Tony said, "and tell me everything you did that I would have told you not to. In order. Officially."

"Dad—"

"With dinner. The full ranks. Whatever Happy made for you while I was away on — paperwork. Steaks, chicken, cheeseburgers for the kid who inherited it honestly, whatever is here. I ate—" the face went briefly complicated somewhere behind the parts already decided not to show this week "—nothing I want to talk about, let me put it that way, and I want you to have options. Go."

Mark went. Dad put the good arm across Pepper's shoulders like a walking reentry and let Happy ease the door, and the cliff house received the entire catalogue at once, and the water completed its afternoon reports the way water does when nothing else requires it.

*PANEL FIVE — THE WORKSHOP, DEEP NIGHT*

Pepper found him at mid-dark, awake on the workshop couch in the lamplight, staring at nothing with the exhausted, high-focus attention of a man holding an assembly.

"You should be sleeping," she said. "You flew around the planet today. You gave a press conference. You scared the stock market into a state that requires work."

"Can't sleep," Tony said, dry. "Doc's lodged a complaint. He has technical terms." He sat up a little. "Pep. I need you to see something. You're going to want me to have told you before." He opened the work shirt. The work shirt he'd been rediscovering all evening went deliberately plain — and whatever it covered had been reported to no one. This was not plain: there was a ring of cold blue light at the center of Tony Stark's sternum where the sternum had always been, lens of it burning softly into the dim — a hole in him, lit. The wire joined a coil. The world joined him in staring.

"Oh my god," Pepper said, very quietly. "Tony."

"It's—" — he found the floor with his free hand's measure, the way he'd steadied himself on sand under a shredded canopy a month out of the desert, "—the only thing keeping the shrapnel from my heart. In the desert it was a car battery." A pause, the joke upfront but still holding. "I'm in an upgrade. Miniaturized. I made it from junk in a cave. Bottle caps. A frankly palladium episode. Details tomorrow."

"A cave," Pepper said.

"You're getting the whole tour tomorrow," Tony said. "Tonight I need you to—" the shirt went the rest way, grim-decisive, "— it's got to come out. The old coil's already turning itself into battery jam. I need another pair of hands and steady ones. You."

"I—" Pepper had handled his schedule for years, his boardroom, his consequences, his obituary pieces in draft. She had, at no point in the official ledger, handled his chest. "Okay. Talk me through it."

"So— take it easy," Tony said, and his grin was, at last, almost his own. "You're going to put your hand in. There. In the hole. There's a wire. You keep your olive fingers away from it or we have a retrospective."

It went slowly, in blue-buried breaths: her hand inside the light, his own hand steadying her wrist with the small, urgent, practical care of a man who had not yet accounted for himself entirely to anyone on earth. Her face was a work. The lamp was a work. Nothing else spoke.

"Don't—" she said, tight, when the whole coil came loose at last with the slow suction of a stuck jar lid, "—ask me to do surgery on you ever again."

"Never again," Tony said, breathing, sweat come up on his forehead and down off his cheek like weather. "That's the one thing. The — " the new core seated home in a glow of softer hymn, its new current clearing his lungs in waves, "— except the one where you're the only one who can."

Pepper looked at the old palladium core in her palm, dim, spent, a heartbeat's weight. She looked at the new one glowing in his chest like a promise the size of a lighthouse keeping itself.

"I'm going to have this engraved," she said. "And I'm going to put it on a plaque. Proof."

"Of what," Tony said.

"That **Tony Stark has a heart.**"

Upstairs, in a dream of warm deep water with his hands in it, the boy slept, and did not know.

The lamp in his room kept its age-old unchanged blue.

*PANEL SIX — THE BENCH, NEXT MORNING*

Tony stood at the corner of the dusty sheet with his good hand on his hip, looking at the white shape under it the way he used to look at the elephants he'd designed and hadn't personally sourced. Morning came level off the ocean, caught the film scraps in the tray beside it — solar-thin, wing-pale — and Mark, at the secondary bench with the bolts laid out like children at choir, refused to fill the silence first.

"How big," Tony said.

"Full span about—" the boy shaped it with his hands. "She's built to carry her own weight in sun and nothing else. The battery was bad. I accepted that. I fitted the trauma kit, the thermal blanket, the water, some small medical—" his voice had settled into the engineer-channel, white-box steady at regulation height, "—things for staying warm and holding location, mostly. In case anyone was found."

"In case anyone was found," Tony said, not finishing it any other way.

The ocean completed reports.

"She doesn't have weapons," Mark said. "That was the first law. Nothing that hurts."

"Good boy," Tony said, quiet, old-blooded, and put the good hand flat on top of the sheet without lifting it — feeling the wing ribs through the dust-cloth the way a priest touches things, honored-his-time, and did not draw it back. Because that was faith too. Because trusting what his son had spent the summer building so the boy could grieve with penknife discipline was family property too. Trust him in the shop. Wait. There would be time.

The hand lifted.

"I built something to find you," Mark said. "It didn't."

"You built something that loves me," Tony said. "That's the one that found me first."

Mark looked at him for several full seconds, and there was no notebook in the world that wanted half of what passed between them then.

"It's grounded," he said finally. "Torn film. She's mending herself, she's somewhere safe out of the way, she'll wait. Nothing is finished. I have notes."

"I have notes too, kid. Mostly bad ones." Tony leaned in to let the good hand cover the head under the jaw the way it always landed: rough, brief, family-key. The ribs' editorial comment returned one half-hitch before the lie could muffle it; Mark saw the wince and filed it in the drawer where the sleeve knots had taught the genre. "When either of us wants to compare, we'll compare. Not this week. Deal? Raincheck."

"Deal," Mark said.

"Then feed a man. Rhodey's sending steak. The board's going to send—" Tony paused, catastrophically casual, the way he announced icebergs. "Obie's coming later. Because Obie. And you'll do the thing you do."

"Dad—"

"Be the sane one," Tony said, quoting himself across months and years and deserts, and walked out of the workshop with the walk of a man who had decided limping would not be reported.

*PANEL SEVEN — THE PARLOR, LATER*

Obadiah came to dinner the way he always came: peppermints and bulk and gratitude announced, the family coat settling over the dining room.

The table talk traveled through beef and stock-price and the board the way world-traffic travels through weather. Pepper did not look up when the ask came — the ask was all polished family doing duty's agonized face, long-practiced, the hesitation designed down to the exact door-sill of the tone: *what we stand for. the shareholders. one photo before the next quarter's accounting.* Room-trick: it was meant for Dad's boy, staged inside Dad's tiredness — and it was, technically, reasonable.

Mark heard every floor of it arrive. The sensing ran with new bandwidth under it, nothing of the old childship left hanging on. Surface: warm gravitas, duty. Second: calculated force put where Dad wouldn't look for it. Third: worry still real — the company genuinely was in attrition, nothing counterfeit about the storm. And a fourth thing, ribboning slower through the register: watching *Mark himself,* calibrated-care without menace — insider-care, ledger-care, the way a family estate looks long at the small spare piece of itself after the main instrument has wobbled.

The door in his head said only: *I see all of it. just not yet what it means.*

"Uncle Obie," Mark said, and twelve years' worth of very careful came into the words wearing an ordinary boy's face. "The press is full of Dad being thinner and the company bleeding. I'll give you one on the driveway — one step below the choke point, no podium, no boards behind us — if you stop doing the far-desk face like it costs you something to sit and eat with the family you claim we're all in."

Tony laughed — sudden, off-guard, genuinely delighted — and pulled it back short when the ribs objected, but the warmth had already gone around the table, and the evening recalibrated around it.

"The kid," Tony said, flat and full. "I sat in the desert for three months having serious conversations with myself about discipline. And he's out here at twelve doing joint maneuvers on the optics of when the board would stop bleeding."

Obadiah's face moved around the joke from inside — the layers sliding their half-beat shift in warm colors, the sheet of family-suffering settling back across the board — and Mark folded the lens shut without taking anything home: *I can't read what that move was.* No conclusion arrived. Fine. Notes for later.

"Fair," Obadiah said, at last, and the big man's face had flexed into something maybe nearer sincere than warm. "Driveway, one step below the choke point. Done. You're getting older, boy." He raised the glass. "Your father's lucky. We're all—" the glass came round the table, leveled "—lucky."

"The luck belongs to the house," JARVIS said quietly from the ceiling, and Obadiah — most reliable of all — raised the glass half an inch in the direction of the voice.

---

The night came down slower now, blue and brick-thick: Dad home one wall away, the house's lamps starting to burn for the other reason, the ocean doing its reports. Mark sat in bed at the late hour with the notebook and the lamp and the dust-file on the drone shelf, and let the week exhale through his hands.

*entry twenty-three. Dad came home. ribs hurt. guard-up changed. warm went deep — all the way down. nothing lean. nothing shadowed. finished.*

*rock: paid in memory, not in rock. the joke stands. next time he tells it I'll say I heard it from Pepper.*

*bench: he put his hand on the sheet. honored it. didn't lift it. trusts the shop. I'd do that for him. someday I'll lift it myself.*

*Obie: watching me now. carefully, no malice in it I can reach. hotter than warm, colder than cold, deeper than shallow. no name for it. noted.*

*Albatross: mended, hiding, drinking sun, waiting out of coalition weather. later.*

*dream fragments: two more nights. water at depth, my hands, light moving behind them. no proof. observer only. same law.*

Under that, in the kid margin where he wrote the memos that were actually prayers:

*we got him back changed. guard-up is new. love is old. both true at once are hard to write and easy to hold.*

*one more note. the lamp sees all of it anyway.*

The lamp kept its slow blue weather for him one more whole night, and down the hall the door-cracks said Dad was awake too, the rooms breathing around what it cost a built man to hold still in one safe place. Balancing. Both of them. In the hush.

"Love you too," Mark whispered into it, into the lamp, into the room, into the other side of the sleep they were both not-quite reaching.

And slept — because this time there was a body on the other side of the ocean for it, and there were lamps, and lamps were enough light to go home by.
