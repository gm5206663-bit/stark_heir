The siege learned the house's shapes.

It learned the gate first — the vans returning each morning like habits, lenses following the light — and then it learned the beach path, and then a helicopter that wasn't theirs started keeping a polite circle out over the water, far enough to be deniable. Mark learned to hear it without looking: heavyweight twin by chord, pressure-wash of the blades over the cliff line, the specific lazy orbit of a camera crew trying not to get quoted. He could tell it from the coast guard by the patience.

"Channel two is back," JARVIS said, mornings, with the misery of a butler reading the weather.

"Over the water?"

"Over the water, circle's legal."

"Then it's weather," Mark said, and let it be weather — and that was the first small new thing, the one nobody was told about because there was nothing to tell: the noise of the world had started to arrive with a dial attached. At the gate, when the shouting hit — *Mark! How's your mother! How do you feel!* — he let his attention do what it had learned to do in Obadiah's study, the way you stop pressing an ear to a door, and the hail of it went from skin to weather. It cost him a headache after. It was worth a headache.

The notebook said, entry four: *paper doesn't move when I ask. nothing. noted.*

Under it: *the dial works. I have an off switch in crowds. nobody knows where I keep it.*

Statement day came with flags.

Stark Industries put Obadiah Stane on the steps of the tower under a sky the color of polished boardroom, and the whole country watched him be steady. Mark watched from the workshop bench, solder cooling, one shoulder turned to the screen.

"—every resource," Obadiah said, voice thick in exactly the right places. "Every channel. We will not rest. Tony Stark is more than a colleague to me — he's family. His son is my family. I spoke to the boy this week — he's holding up, he's brave, and I want the world to know the Stark house is standing."

"He means the company," JARVIS said quietly.

"I know what he means." Mark didn't look up. "He's not wrong, either. We're standing."

That evening Obadiah called the house line, and Mark took it in the kitchen with Pepper listening over the lip of her wine glass.

"Son." Warm gravel, real-sounding, family-key. "The board's asked me to ask. One photo. You and me on the steps, holding hands — nothing said, nothing sold. Two minutes. It would steady a lot of people who need steadying right now."

Mark looked at Pepper. Pepper's face had gone very calm, which meant the opposite.

*Two minutes of calm,* the surface said. *Two minutes of optics,* said the layer underneath — and underneath that, stranger and newer, a third thing his sensing had only just gotten tall enough to hear: worry that was real, all the way down, collector's-grade. Uncle Obie was worried. About the company, about the board, worry braided through the performance like thread through cloth — real worry wearing the shape of family concern, family concern wearing the shape of duty, all of it true and none of it simple.

"Uncle Obie." Twelve years old, voice polite and level. "I'm the heir a thousand days a year. Can I just be the kid this month?"

A pause. A laugh, affectionate, bruised. "Fair, son. Fair. Stay strong in there, huh? Your dad would be—" the breath went somewhere and came back. "He'd be proud of you."

"Yeah," Mark said, and hung up, and sat in the kitchen quiet while Pepper finished her wine standing up.

"You did good," Pepper said finally.

"I felt how worried he is," Mark said. "The worry's real. The hands holding it are his."

Pepper looked at him for a long moment, the way adults look when they've decided not to say the thing. "Eat something," she said. "Rhodey called while you were in the shop. He said—" she found the exact words in her own face. "'Tell the kid no news is every kind of news, and I'm still looking, and eat something.'"

"Tell him I'm eating something."

"He said you'd say that," Pepper said. "I told him I'd make sure."

*PANEL — THE CAVE, THE AWAKENING*

He woke to dripping, and a battery.

The dark of the cave had a weight and a temperature, and under both of those, a smell: rock dust, fuel, old smoke, new blood. Tony Stark tried to sit up and the world said no with the flat authority of a dropped engine block. Something thick sat in the center of his chest where his chest should have been, wired to a car battery on a wooden crate.

"Easy." The voice was accented, calm, unhurried — the voice of a man who had made peace with a hard world one careful correction at a time. Yinsen, he would say later, this man with the tired hands and the surviving glasses, working in the lamplight with strips of cloth like the whole affair was ordinary. "Easy. You will tear it out, and I spent my best work on you. I have seen many wounds like yours. Most of them on the dead."

"What—" Tony's own voice was gravel in a tin can. "What is—" he looked down. The middle of him was a junction box. "—this."

"Most of the metal, I pulled out. The small pieces, the ones the knife could not find, they are marching. Toward your heart." Yinsen angled the lamp so Tony could see the magnet sunker in his own sternum like a second, ill-born organ. "This — this is an electromagnet. It keeps the shrapnel from your heart. It is—" the careful smile, apologetic hands, "—powered by a car battery, which I am sorry to say is the best engineering available in this zip code, if this place had zip codes."

Tony looked at the wires running out of himself like vines out of an old house. Looked at the battery. Looked at the walls, rough-hewn, camera-eyed, echoing with men somewhere beyond a heavy door who measured him in rooms of iron.

"Okay," Tony said quietly, and maybe he was talking to the battery. "Okay."

---

The bench held the decision the way the bench held everything: in parts, laid out in the order his hands would need them.

He'd stopped trying the paper by then. The paper had won, four entries running, and the night after the fourth failure he'd sat in the blue lamplight and written: *when the ink runs out, build the pen.* Because ink — whatever had snapped a blade guard across a room — ink did not answer questions, did not take requests, did not get on any schedule a twelve-year-old could hold. But the house was full of answers that did.

JARVIS found the blueprint where Mark had started it — a folder called ALBATROSS — at three in the morning, which was also when JARVIS chose to start the argument.

"I count seventeen separate reasons this ends poorly," JARVIS said, ceiling-warm, over the whine of the lathe.

"Give me the top three."

"The first is that you are twelve. The second is that flying an airframe into coalition airspace over an active war zone is the sort of thing nations lose court cases over. The third—" a pause, the human-timed one "—is that your father will kill me."

"Dad will build a second one out of spite." Mark didn't look up from the wing spar. "Fourth?"

"There is no fourth. The first three are load-bearing."

Mark set the spar down, and looked at the ceiling camera the way he'd learned to look at people when it mattered.

"It's not a weapon," he said. "That's the first law. It carries nothing that hurts anything. It flies, it waits, it drinks sunlight, it listens, it carries water and a trauma kit and a thermal blanket and an injector, and if it ever finds—" the breath; let it come; let it go "—then the worst thing it does to anyone is land nearby and be warm. And nobody gets to see my ends of it. You fly the weather, I fly the bird, we keep the bird honest."

"And the neuro-link," JARVIS said, evenly.

And there it was — the heart of it, laid between them like the last part on the bench.

The house knew his head better than any hospital on earth. It knew it from the slumber years, from the special-disease years, from years of ceilings full of careful medical attention: the scalp nets they'd fitted him at age seven, month upon month of readings nobody had ever quite explained, the couch in the workshop's med corner printed with the shape of his sleeping body, the archive of ten thousand hours of *Mark Stark, asleep, breathing, wrong, unexplainable.* He'd read that archive at ten the way other kids read forbidden comics.

He knew neuro-bridges the way other kids knew bicycle chains.

"The link is how I fly it," Mark said. "Hand controllers are for drones, JARVIS. This one's mine. I want the air."

A long silence — about two seconds, which for JARVIS was a season.

"Guardrails," JARVIS said. "Non-negotiable. Your reflexes travel at the speed of the satellite; mine travel at the speed of you. Twitch-class corrections are mine. If the link runs you past medical lines, I pull it, and you swear — between us, not on paper — that you let it go."

"I swear between us," Mark said.

"Then we build it to lose well," JARVIS said. "Definition: if this ends with you walking away disappointed, we have won."

"And if it works?"

A pause, softer. "Then your father gets a better class of rescue than I was built to offer. Grab the spar, Mark. Hold it steady. We're going to need the long clamps."

The first one crashed into the pool.

It was called mk-I, and it crashed because cliff wind lies, and because Mark, synced for the first real session, had made the universal mistake of flying a bird with a boy's body still attached somewhere else — vertigo folding him over the bench mid-hover, gut rolling, the link's borrowed horizon tipping impossible directions, so that he retched into the wastebasket at the exact moment the airframe stalled; and it went down like a white prayer into the deep end and steamed politely.

Happy stood at the workshop door with a towel and an expression Mark had last seen after the motorcycle cliff argument.

"That," Happy said, "was loud."

"I know where every piece is," Mark said, pale, wiping his mouth. "It's a pool. JARVIS, retrieve everything above the waterline."

"Already submerged commands acknowledged." A sardonic motor whine rose from the pool lights. "Shall I bill it to R and D?"

"Bill it to 'Mark, misc.' That's the account this family runs on."

"You're drenched and you puked," Happy said, crossing the shop floor, "and you're arguing with the ceiling at noon. Your dad used to do that. You're going to need food and you're not going to ask for it, so I'm saying it so it's said: food. Then the pool, JARVIS, not Mark."

"I—" Mark's stomach rolled its objection. "I had a — vertigo. From the sync. Doesn't happen twice."

"The vertigo doesn't happen twice," JARVIS agreed, tone careful. "It happens differently, for longer, or not at all. Unknown until we test. Session tolerance, first: forty minutes to nausea. Logged."

"Then we eat first next time and log that too," Mark said — and heard himself, and grinned despite the pale, because this was what he had instead of ink: *things that answered.* Test, log, adjust, repeat. The Talent's oldest job, done with hands.

The bolt blink came the next afternoon, undramatic as grace.

A solder slip — his finger jerked, a run of heat across the bench, the tiny four-mil bolt skittered off the edge — and then it didn't. It hung, at the bench's lip, for maybe half a second. Maybe less. Mark absolutely did not breathe. It dropped. The shop hummed. Nothing else in the building noticed.

After a moment, and not quickly, more like a man documenting weather than a boy claiming a miracle, he picked up the pen for the notebook that had learned to carry the truth.

*entry six. solder slipped. bolt stopped at the bench lip for a blink. did not ask it to. cannot repeat it. hungrier than the drop justifies. logged anyway.*

Underneath, after thinking: *it's not ink. it's not nothing. keep the pen moving.*

*PANEL — THE CAVE, THE DEMAND*

They brought him the photographs at gunpoint, spread on the steel table like a menu.

The Jericho. White and fat and shameless on its launcher, mid-salem at the test range — his machine, his morning, his first page of the trip's itinerary, back when the itinerary had a Tuesday. Raza's men held him at the shoulders, not hard yet, the way men arrange furniture they expect to move again. Raza walked the rim of the table without touching it, a buyer trying to keep his wanting reasonable.

The offer, translated through the interpreter's soft careful vowels, was what offers in that cave always were: build the Jericho, here, with our materials, and you will be set free. Freedom, pronounced over the photographs, like a word that had never once meant itself in this place.

Tony looked at his own machine looking back at him from ten photographs.

"I refuse," Tony said.

They took him out into the yard and did it the way the yard did things. Board. Bucket. The afternoon above the rim of the gorge very blue, very distant, an entire separable heaven stacked out of reach over the mouths of his own weapons' crates. They held him under and let him count the sky, and after a time he understood that the counting was the point: the time you thought you had was the first thing they took, and the seconds of it after that were entirely yours to negotiate with.

When they brought him back inside, he sat on the floor of the cell for a while and said nothing at all, and the wires went softly with his breathing, and Yinsen — who had been waiting, who had seen the yard before — brought him a cup of water and set it within reach without commentary.

"They will kill you either way," Yinsen said, not unkindly. "Tomorrow, or when the work is finished. You know this."

"Yeah," Tony said.

"This is their custom," Yinsen said. "The promise is for the workers. Not the work."

Tony looked at the steel table the photographs had left empty. Looked at the dark mouth of the forge they had let these men build for their Jericho.

"Then we build," Tony Stark said, and it was the first thing he said in that cave that sounded whole.

By the second month the house had routines it didn't talk about.

The sessions ran long and honest: forty minutes became an hour and a half, then three, the way a body learns water — slowly, then saying the water was always the point. The bones-deep exhaustion after every Link came regular as a tide, and the appetite after that, and then the longer sleeps, dark and rich and recovery-shaped, that JARVIS logged with the quiet reverence of an archivist: *Mark consolidated. Body paid. Body paid. Body paid.*

He grew.

Pepper put a pencil line on the pantry frame on the first of the month and then another, higher, exasperated: "You're doing this on purpose now."

"I'm doing biology on purpose. It's the only machine I can run where nobody needs me to be anything."

The helicopter over the water stopped being weather in week six and started being information: light civilian twin, blades at that particular shallow-disc angle, carbon-fiber unit by the brightness of the note — a company model Mark had logged two summers back, same crew, same circuit, same polite illegality. He wrote it down and didn't bother looking up.

The screw-hold came in month two's tail end, the way it always came: not when he was asking.

The workshop light had gone amber toward evening; the bench was laid with the mk-II's port wing; he reached for a dropped M-three screw rolling toward the floor vent, and without deciding anything about it — without ink, without prayer — he just *had* it, mid-roll, an inch off the plenum tile, three rounded seconds of total disciplineless science fiction hanging there in the amber light, perfectly still, the screw reflecting his own dark pupil while his breathing counted itself.

It fell. His stomach turned over hard enough to make him sit down on the stool, something deeper than food-money gone out of the register again, and his palms were cold and shiny.

"Noted?" JARVIS said quietly. He'd had the camera on. He'd been watching since the bolt.

"Noted." Mark let himself breathe. "State: deliberate adjacency. Not summoned. It happens when I'm not asking. Duration: three seconds, then the cost. Cost: nausea." He rubbed his arms. The classroom he was giving himself: *observe, don't claim.* "It grows when I'm not watching it. Tell me that's growing, JARVIS. Not imagining."

"Duration trend line is positive," JARVIS said. "Frequency: rare. Statistical fight-worthiness: laughable. Discipline-worthiness: you already wrote it down before I finished the sentence." A beat. "That's the metric that matters to me, Mark. The part where your first instinct was the notebook."

The room went warm after that. Pepper wandered in around that time with a file folder, stopped at the door, saw the wing and the streaks of solder-sweat on Mark's forearm and the particular nothing-special posture he performed around secrets, and stood on the threshold for a while without saying any of the several things visible on her face.

"You're disappearing into the bench," she said finally. "I don't mean I worry. I mean: what are you eating."

"Happy feeds me."

"Happy doesn't know about half of this." She came in; her shoes went quiet on the shop floor the way they did when she was in the mood to be careful. "Can I ask what it is, at the level at which a person who loves you gets an answer?"

Mark looked up at her. She'd said *a person who loves you* like it was a job title, because for this house it was.

"I'm building something that looks," he said, steady, carefully choosing the room the words would be allowed to light up. "Not a weapon. I can't tell you how it works. If it works, you'll know I'm okay. If I tell you more now, you'll have to lie about it, and I won't do that to you."

Pepper regarded him for a very long time — the third-level regard, where what you see is the other person deciding whether the truth you've told them is enough truth for the relationship.

"Those are the wheels this family runs on," she said, and left it at that, and left.

He'd grown an inch and lost half a breath of childhood, and he could feel the trade happening, could feel the particular thrifty Olympian accounting the body kept when it matters most. The Long Clamps took the mk-II the way they'd taken everything. The solar film came off the roll pale as breath over water. The fuel cell was small and honest and nowhere near enough — the company's reactor was a building, not a battery, and buildings don't fly — which was the truth his father had been born not to say and his son had been born to say for a decade of slumber-years' worth of knowing any body he's ever used has a limit.

*power is the weakness,* the notebook said. *accept it. design around it. albatross logic: the weakness is also the promise. light is free. wind is free. she goes far because she carries nothing, not even her own weight if possible.*

He named her for the bird that sleeps on the wing.

*PANEL — THE CAVE, THE WEEKS*

The cave became, by degrees and by hands, two things at once.

For the camera above the heavy door, it became a Jericho workshop: crates marked with his own company's stencils opened under guard, missile bodies laid out for inspection, copper requisitioned in spools that the guards logged on their clipboard and never once understood. For Yinsen and Tony, in the hours the camera's eye rested, it became a machine shop with no name: missile casings cut with torch and patience, a forge powered by firedamp and pirated charge, a crucible that did not announce what it held.

Yinsen kept the ledgers for their keepers and the questions for himself, and the questions came on the night they played the board game across the upturned crate, Yinsen's patient fingers moving brown and ivory with unhurried competence.

"Do you have a family?" Yinsen asked.

"Me? No." Tony moved a piece, badly, on purpose, feeling the ridiculous animal warmth that came with admission. "I'm on my own. I mean — " the piece stopped in his fingers, and the cave's whole sound of men and water and echo went past him and came back altered, "— I have a son. Twelve. I have a son, and I — " the wire hummed at his sternum like a second pulse making its point, "— I'm not on my own at all. Never have been. It's just the only thing I ever actually swore to and I—

The pause finished itself. Yinsen did not look up from the board.

"And yours?" Tony said, asking first, because for once in his pampered engineered life he knew to ask first.

"Mine are—" Yinsen moved his piece. Finished his turn. Spoke softly, past the question of another game, "— waiting for me. In Gulmira. I will see them, when I leave this place."

"In Gulmira."

"Yes," Yinsen said, simply. "In Gulmira."

The weeks went on. The suit that was not a Jericho grew on its racks like a secret put together from soup cans and flensing knives — the hands of it first, because a man needs his hands, then the body around the battery's leaner descendant — the new core, the small bright circle Tony forged from missile scrap and palladium and stubbornness, the thing that hummed so beautifully into his wound that Yinsen held up the lamp with both hands and stared.

"This—" Yinsen whispered.

"This," Tony said, "is upgraded."

It put out a light like a low sun inside a tin cup. The guards in the yard, watching their clipboard's promised Jericho grow in familiar shapes, noted nothing that mattered.

*OVER THE GRID, KUNAR — MONTH TWO*

The search line ran north to south today, same as yesterday, same as the week before that: low and slow and shadowed by a sun that did not care about any of it.

Colonel James Rhodes sat the right seat of the rescue bird with the desert unspooling under the skids in every shade there was and no shade that mattered. Behind him the medics kept the kit ready the way professionals keep hope ready — stowed, inventoried, unspoken about. Below, wadis drew their thin unreadable writing across the rock, and the rock kept all its answers the way rock does.

"Anything?" Rhodes asked, the way he'd asked it every sortie, the way you ask a question that has become a form of prayer.

"Wreck trace at grid four is the old one, sir. Nothing new since the site pass. No thermal, no movement, no signals but the usual." A pause. "It's been weeks, Colonel."

"I know how long it's been." His eyes kept working the ground. He'd flown this desert in exercises; he'd chased Tony Stark across casinos in four countries; he'd carried the man's award out of Caesar's in his dress blues. "Keep the line. We go grid five, we turn at the ridge, we come home. Same as always."

"And tomorrow, sir?"

Rhodey looked at the empty co-pilot seat for one second, the way a man looks at a chair a friend is supposed to fill.

"Tomorrow is grid six," he said. "We don't stop. That's the whole job. We don't stop."

The launch was at four in the morning, because the best launch windows were at four in the morning and because Mark had learned that grief and excitement both smelled like solder at four in the morning.

The Albatross climbed out on her rotors, silent as a held breath, all her cost accounted: solar film on the wing, cells in the spine, fuel reserve in her small honest tank, the medical bay in her belly stocked like a chapel. At altitude the rotors folded back into the body, the solar film woke, and the falcon settled into her glide across the first fat immensity of the Pacific.

Mark flew her from the workbench couch, the scalp net cool around his temples, the room blue with lamp-light and monitor glow. The link had ripened across the month from a clumsy borrowed horizon into something nearer to what he'd wanted it to be: not a window. A second body. The air around the wings came as touch on skin he didn't have; the camera feeds arrived as sight from eyes that lived in fresh places; the satellite delay ran at the speed of honey, liquid patience, forcing his reach — his psychic reach, the Talent's oldest friend — to slow down enough to actually meet the bird's own balance. JARVIS rode the twitches in the background like a hand under an elbow.

"She's going to make the sky," Mark whispered, toward midnight of the first day, exhaustion molasses-sweet in his bones.

The counselors said grief was the worst kind of training. The researchers said the body would learn what it needed. Mark's body learned the way he flew the bird: at the speed of honey, and then at the speed of the ocean.

Below him, across whatever enormous layered mystery eleven time zones carry, the ocean went.

The lean happened on the third sortie.

The grid over Kunar had taken its shape from the files he'd built: search lines from the news reports' coordinates, convoy-site imagery on his private fold, the terrain data JARVIS had wrangled where wrangling existed. Tag, mark, resume the next line. On the third sortie the bird hung at her altitude over the desert, thermal and optical stitching the wadi shadow-lines, her passive RF ears held quiet — and something behind the wrongness leaned.

Not at the wadi. Not at any reading. Just — lean. A direction. The way water wants a downhill. The sort of thing a boy who had built his whole being on the strictest possible accounting would want to throw the whole air force at, and did not.

*entry seventeen.* Mark's hand was steady. His voice was not. *the wrongness leans. it has leaned three times, always the same way. it is not a wire. it is not proof. it is possibly my imagination doing what imagination does. lean is not a certificate. obey your own law. note and keep flying the grid.*

He kept flying the grid.

The convoy site came and went below as the light turned: the scorch tracks thermography-pale, decomposition mapping as honest death, everything the search had already found — nothing the world didn't know, nothing it could not see from its orbit. He reviewed and logged and wrote *no new knowledge* like a man tattooing it on.

Then the rage came, or the sky brought it: a dust storm blown in hard from the northeast at the edge of vision, a boil of brown erasing the horizon's arithmetic, and the Albatross — light, patient, too far from a hangar, too proud to retreat from something she could have ridden out — took a microburst full in the wing film and settled, wounded, into a ridge desert crease, dust-haloed, tilted like a dropped handkerchief. Repair requirements per JARVIS: she gave her own estimate — landed status, mandrel arms deployed, spare film in her spine, two hours of her own slow wilderness surgery before she could lift again. She'd been built to mend herself on a ridge for exactly this, and she was, as always, alone with her own inventory.

Mark sat back from the link in the lamp-blue dark, heart hard, hands shaking, sweat cold, body calculating costs it had taught him to speak. Every sheet of solar film on the bench was accounted. Every tool in the shop was leased. Every hour the bird stayed grounded was an hour nobody was looking from her eyes, and the wrongness leaned toward the southeast, and it leaned again, and the wrongness was not a wire, and the wrongness was not a wire, and it was not a wire, and it was —

"Every rule I have," Mark said aloud, to the blue, "ends with 'obey your own law.'"

"That is correct," JARVIS said. "Because you wrote all the rules. That's what a discipline looks like from the inside. Take the session off. Eat. I will handle the recovery list."

He did not get the film fully cut before Pepper's voice came breaking through the house.

*PANEL — THE CAVE, THE ESCAPE*

Raza learned faster than a shallow man should learn. That was the only thing that rushed them.

The rush snapped down when the progress bar stalled at four-fifths and the pounding came on the door: *open now, off the schedule, this is not the schedule* — and Yinsen did the thing his tattered careful soul had been deciding for weeks: he took up a rifle from the rack of the lesser guard, and he stepped into the doorway, and he bought a genius time with the only currency left in him.

"Yinsen—"

"Finish it," Yinsen said, without looking back. "I will hold—" and he was already out into the torchlight, ten-ring shouting and ten-ring noise.

The suit woke in beats. Feet first. Then hands. Then spine. The small sun inside it steadied, clearing its throat in palladium, and the boots of Iron Man Mark One broke the first chain of commandment of the cave — the door, itself — flat off its hinges like the first argument of the opened century.

Tony found Yinsen under the rubble at the corridor's last corner, glasses gone gray, breath coming thin and measured, with the ten-ring's drawn knife raked somewhere past mention across his chest. The rifle lay near his hands. The hands lay open near his heart.

"Thank you for saving me," Tony said — and the words came out rough and panicked because the suit did not have a face, it had a voice, and his voice was the only thing the armor could hold open.

"Do not." Yinsen's eyes found the small light burning circles in Tony's new chest. Pain did not seem to have veto over him anymore. "You saved me. You're my—" breath, carefully rationed, "—proof. You are the proof."

Tony saw the board game with the brown and ivory in the back of his mind. Gulmira. Waiting.

"Don't—" Tony's hand was an oversize iron paw against the rope of Yinsen's wrist, checking for the good part of life that was still in there. "Don't die, okay? We have a plan. We go. We—"

"This was always the plan," Yinsen said. "For me. I have—none left." His eyes went over the iron shoulder, to the far wall of the cave, and past the wall, to Gulmira. "You have more to do. More family. Go find them." A pause; more breath, rationed like everything on the man's ledger had always been, generous in paucity. "Don't— waste it. This life. Okay?"

The little sun in Tony's chest hummed. The cave rang with men coming. Yinsen closed his eyes.

The rest of the escape went loud, and the story has already been told by fire and myth: the flame out of the turned arms walking the munitions forward like fire rehearsing its own legend, the last door inward blown wide, the flight more long fall with rockets than flight, the first flight of a man who was not supposed to fly — sky screaming, edge and disintegrating canopy, Mark One hard-landing half a ridge away into the dunes with Tony half-buried in a litter his own wings had shredded.

Tony lay in the sand.

"Okay," Tony told the dunes, and the sun, and the day, and the whole sky that had never been honored like this before. "My turn."

*PANEL — THE DESERT, THE FIND*

The air-cav bird cut low over the dune basin at midmorning, two days after the war zone last publicly dreamed of Tony Stark, and Colonel James Rhodes stood in the door with the wind combing the life back into his uniform and the relief coming up out of a month of mostly buried hope.

The dunes had kept what gulf-winds wouldn't: scorch shadow, burned drapery, one handful of wrecked rocket-and-iron the size of a stalled motorcycle scattered against the sand. And a man. A man in boots he'd earned and fatigues he'd inherited and a look that said he absolutely knew approximately which day of the week it was and one of those answers was wrong.

Rhodey saw, first, the eyes. Then the hand raised, palm up to say — mostly — *please do not shoot me, I have been working.* Then everything else suspended — because Tony Stark, walking toward him in the dust, was sunburned and hollow and sat up like a man building a wall, and was *alive.*

Rhodey's hand was already out of the bird signaling land as his mind caught up to his eyes.

When their hands locked, when the joke he was going to have made started on his face — *how was the fun-vee, sir* — the words didn't fully come. The rotor wind beat its clock around them.

"Next time," Rhodey said, grip holding, "you ride with me."

It came before dawn, on the morning the sky was still grey and the ocean was still deciding about the day, and it came down the house speakers the way it had to come, because JARVIS had been listening for exactly that wire since before Mark was born into this family:

"Rhodey," Pepper said, and her voice would not come out of professional for love or money, "—they found him. Rhodey's helicopter. They—" the air shifted; she stopped; the whole house stopped with her. "Mark. Mark. They found him."

In the workshop, Mark sat very still.

The wrongness did not reply. The wrongness had never replied. The wrongness leaned toward the southeast and probably always would; he didn't care; the wrongness wasn't a wire. But the wire, the real wire, was coming down the speakers, now, and it was Pepper, who never lied to him, who'd said *you're the house* with the gravity people use for things that are true forever, and the house held, and the house held —

He wasn't going to find Dad.

He hadn't found him.

Someone else had carried his father out of the desert on the side of a chopper in the long third month, while he sat on a ridge crease in his second body with a torn wing like a dropped handkerchief, knowing nothing, donating nothing of what could have been gifted — because the bird was down when the sky burned, and the sky burned while the bird was down, and no ledger of casualties in any war ever cared how hard you tried.

Mark got up. His bones felt like water and iron. His heart was a hammer that had finally found the nail that belonged to it. He went to the covered bench in the corner — the white shape under the dust sheet, the shape only the house knew — and stood there with his hand on the wing through the cloth, breathing.

*I didn't find him.*

*I built wings anyway.*

*The wings work.*

Pepper was at the door, half-dressed for the airport, tears openly marching down a face that did not usually open, and she saw Mark — face wrecked, hand on the dust sheet, alive, twelve, taller than she remembered him being — and stopped in the doorway with the particular respect she paid to spaces where the actual congregation was meeting.

"He's alive," Pepper said, as if it wasn't finished yet, as if someone inside her had not finished saying it. "He's alive, Mark."

"I know," Mark said, voice breaking across his face, and started toward the door, and stopped dead, and looked back.

At the bench. At the bird. At the ridge a world away. At the whole failed instrument of rescue that would sit on that ridge in the wind for as long as the world's wind would carry it.

*Bring me my rock from Kunar, Dad,* the notebook said, heartbeat steady, ink dry. *The wings will be waiting.*

He went to his father.
