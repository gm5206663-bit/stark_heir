*PANEL ONE — THE STORM AFTER, AND THE FIRST FRIDAY*

The morning after the chest-light and the plaque promise, the country woke up busy on Stark Industries' dime. The kitchen television stacked opinions in rows: men in flag pins calling the shutdown a genius's nervous breakdown, women with calmer voices calling it a man taking his name back in public. The word *irresponsible* got said until it lost its teeth. The word *heritage* got said until it found some. In the crawl at the bottom, the stock number slid like weather from a bad country.

Mark ate his eggs slower than the crawl could fall.

Above the set, the house went on about its ordinary breathing. The espresso machine cleared its throat. The pool beyond the glass kept its own patient weather. JARVIS moved behind the wires with the hush of a butler quietly clearing ashtrays. Somewhere under his sneakers — under the slab, under the long tool halls — something was already moving. Mark checked the way he had been taught to check first: ordinary means. Ordinary means gave him a sealed basement and a father who left for breakfast and didn't come back up.

Tony came up at noon with the sling folded over one shoulder like a flag he intended to resign from. Whatever the surgeons had recommended had been crumpled somewhere out of sight. He killed the anchors with a thumb, found the carafe empty, and poured the coffee that belonged, officially, to JARVIS's schedule.

"Are you going to tell them," Mark said. "The board. The newsrooms. Tell them what the shutdown actually is."

"Tell them what, exactly?" Tony looked over the mug's rim with the wiser-than-thou innocence of a man who has never successfully owned a houseplant. "That I met a man in a desert who taught me arithmetic I should have learned at a shareholder meeting? That we're going to build something else now — in public, in real life — and let the world chew on it slowly?"

"They're chewing already."

"Let them. Chewers burn out." Tony's gaze went past the crawl, out to the cliffs, where the sea was paying attention to no one's market value. "Here's the order of operations, kid. You walk. Then you run. You say the words in order, because you don't get to skip."

That was the whole of the morning sermon. The week folded itself into evenings — and by Friday, the first standing law of the new house had been ratified.

It arrived at seven exactly in two grease-slick paper bags, onto the island counter that had survived worse legal frameworks than this. Happy had his own chair and his own sack at the far end, peace terms included. Fries opened the session. The honorees came out in napkins as heavy as parchment, the lettuce staging rebellions, the cheese holding its structure purely on faith. Tony raised his cheeseburger like a gavel and delivered a speech of seven words:

"This stands. Fridays. Whenever we're both home."

"Second," Mark said, raising his. The quorum was two and the motion carried, the way every motion that mattered in this house carried — under grease, under laughter, under all the filed-down things neither of them would ever file.

Later, in his room, with Happy downstairs explaining overtime to an empty kitchen, the notebook took one more line in small disciplined hand: *Fridays stand. No rule is too small to rate a two-chair quorum. Tradition counts as armor.*

*PANEL TWO — THE BENCH*

Down where the warm machinery-sleep pooled, below the slab, the workshop ran the length of the house like the keel of a warship that had been told it was allowed to paint itself. Tony Stark stood at the head of it on the second morning of the rebellion, shirt buttoned all the way because it was that kind of morning, and told a room of holograms what the desert had not been able to tell him.

"Parameters," he said to the air, and JARVIS unfolded steel out of the dark. "One man. Carried. Boots and palms for motive force. Nothing glass where the sky can reach in. No missiles, no racks, no partnership with anyone's targeting computer." The pen hovering over the rotating torso-frame made a brief show of hesitation and then came down. "A platform. Built around the unit I carry. Built to be rebuilt forever."

Sixty-odd projected frames bloomed and spun slowly, like supporters offering themselves for office — legs, waist blocks, shoulder chains — all provisional, none binding. Dummy rolled into the center of the evaluation with the anxiety of a bot who suspected he was the thing being evaluated, and posed.

"Say cheese," Tony told him without looking up. The holo-booth flashed, and the image filed itself under a caption of Tony's choosing: *founding staff, original gang.* "And put the fire extinguisher down. We've got work to do."

---

Time at the bench came apart the way clocks do — bearing by bearing. By the third day there were gauntlets on the tables, repulsor apertures blooming in their palms like hothouse irises. By the fourth, boots stood on the test rig, ceramic-shelled, thrust nozzles seated in the soles. By the fifth, the first boot test happened — in the standard manner of historical disasters: briefly, harmlessly, and permanently on file.

JARVIS counted down with the gravity of a man reading a toast — *boots will power you in three, two, one* — and honest physics decided it was Monday. The boot answered at full temper, and Tony Stark was knocked clean out from under his own feet, flat onto his back in a bloom of fabrication dust — whereupon Dum-E, who had been entrusted with the fire extinguisher against all historical precedent, finally found meaningful employment and emptied it with tenderness directly into his maker's face.

"I *built* this thing," Tony informed the ceiling, foam down to the eyebrows, while Dum-E chirped the small proud note of a paramedic who has saved a life and knows it. "Next time — three percent."

By the end of the week a gauntlet could put a beam through the far wall, and the beam no longer cared which button Tony remembered pressing. The lab had completed the exercise the defense industry had doubted from him for twenty years, which was, stated plainly, the same exercise every time: up. Hover. Land. Adjust. Up again.

Mark came down at ten on Friday with sandwiches, because the new law had quietly annexed the idea that a father who skipped dinner owed the island two grilled cheeses, and found his father holding the left gauntlet up at eye level, watching the light through the palm's iris — small, white-hot, and utterly contained, like a coal behind a grate that knew the household was asleep and had loosened its collar accordingly.

"Flight log one," Tony said to no one in particular, setting the gauntlet down at last. "Date: current. Objective: everything." He thought about it. "JARVIS. Formal. For the record."

"Logged, sir," JARVIS said, gently, as if he had been waiting to be asked since the runway.

*PANEL THREE — RUN BEFORE YOU CAN WALK*

Sunday belonged to the sky. Tony had barred the workshop by unilateral engineer's decree, which in practice meant Mark kept to his window with a textbook he never opened, and the heart-held stillness of a boy whose father had just climbed a ladder that went from the cliff to the edge of the sky.

Out over Point Dume, out over the morning's clean white frame of fog, the gold figure rose with its hands pushed out before it and light falling away from the palms like water through cupped fingers. The coast road went small. The mansions went small. The boats by the canyon went to agreeable little chips on a blue cloth — and the suit held, and turned, and was not falling, and there was a sound in Tony Stark's helmet radio that JARVIS logged with great care as *laughter, spontaneous, sustained.*

"Sir," JARVIS said, dry and courtly, "custom and all known safety literature recommend testing a small altitude before attempting a large one. I would be remiss not to file that formally."

"File it." A banked turn, effortless as a shrug. "Now empty the file. Open skies today, JARVIS. We're going to enjoy ourselves."

"As you say, sir. I note, only as a courtesy, that a rocket has entered airspace under advisement."

"Noted."

The sea's rim swung past. The coastline curled like a signature practicing itself. And because Tony Stark was who he was, the flight did not simply end — it ended with the suit standing on the sky at a comfortable remove above all radar taxonomies, drawing smoke letters with the joy particular to a man who has only just learned that his own name can be written in weather: S — T — A — R — K, patient and huge and already dissolving in the sea's long exhale, though not before half the brunch crowd on the coast road had reason to phone someone about it.

Mark saw the first letter from his window and set the textbook down very carefully. The notebook had rules — observe, don't dramatize; report the fact without the wish — and there was no rule under which *my father writes his name on the city* could be entered without breaking both. He entered it anyway. In the smallest hand he owned: *The sky knew his name today.*

Tony came down off the roof wet-headed and blazing, twenty minutes of sky in his grin, and did nothing about the front of the house except let it listen to him breathe on the stair. Mark met him at the workshop threshold with two grilled cheeses on a plate and a look he had trained in a mirror for weeks to keep from showing.

"How does it feel," Mark asked, "to run."

"Like walking — if walking was something you'd been told for years you weren't cleared for." Tony bit into one of the sandwiches like it had personally signed the defense contract. "You get to come down some nights. When the doors are open. Watch the legs go on."

He did not say: *you are the reason the name's in the sky.* He didn't need to. The suit at the bench, catching the light, was wearing it.

*PANEL FOUR — WHY MARK TWO*

Thursday night, after dinner — Happy's grill night, the tradition's second week, the casino of ketchup unnecessary and undefeated — Mark came down with a homework folder for cover and a question he'd been carrying since the first flight, and forgot the folder on the bench the moment he saw the racks.

Four of them stood along the far wall, given the pale house-light museums keep for the things they love. The first held the cave.

It was cleaned now, rebuilt and honest: plate-iron, welded seams, the long patient ugliness of a thing made by men who expected to die in it — and it was *here,* home, salvage-crate fate exchanged for a place of honor under the lights. On its stand's little brass plate, in stencil:

*MARK ONE.*

The rack beside it held the new figure — clean-lined where the other was brute, gold where the other was gray, palm apertures glowing faintly in standby like night-lights for giants. Its plate read:

*MARK TWO.*

And the third rack, and the fourth, stood empty — docked-frame outlines like pencil ghosts, waiting for whoever came after.

Tony was at the evaporation dishes, up to the wrists in solvent, and he saw Mark see them, and something in his face did the thing it did when equations resolved involuntarily.

"...Hungry?"

*Not tonight,* Mark's stomach said, with surprising dignity. He moved one step further into the room, steadied his voice to cool steel, and asked the question the way you ask the only question:

"Why is it Mark Two."

"What's that?"

"The suits." He didn't look away from the stencils. "The cave's Mark One. The new one's Mark Two. There's empty racks standing there with a family name already on them. So — why. Why do the suits get *my* name."

Silence, down where the machinery-sleep pooled. Tony set the solvent bottle down with exaggerated casualness and did not turn around right away, because this deserved a palm laid flat and time in the light.

"When I built you — " he stopped, because the sentence had been drafted wrong for eleven years — "no. When you happened, and I had to say what you were called, everyone had opinions. Family names. Dynasty names. Howard this, Anthony that, names that come with a chair at a table and a debt attached. And I said no." He turned around now, and there was no performance left in it. "I said: the first thing I ever made that made me *more* instead of richer gets its own name, and after that, nothing I make gets a better one. So you're Mark. You're the first. You're the *original*."

He gestured with his chin at the racks — Mark One, Mark Two, the empty docks beyond.

"These are ranked after you. That's not a naming convention. That's the naming *law.* Every suit that ever comes off this bench — every generation, every model, whatever they build over the rest of my life — they honor the boy. Okay?"

*The desert paid for this,* Mark thought, and was very careful with the rule about men going glassy-eyed in shared rooms with minors. Tony's breathing stayed an industry under strict management.

"Okay," Mark said. Carefully.

"Okay."

"Then Mark Two can stay." He let the smile leak through at last, because it was safe now to share the load. "As long as he remembers the name's borrowed."

Tony laughed — big, barrel-shaken, the laugh that boardrooms paid fortunes to be insulted by and never heard half as honestly — and came around the bench and put a hand on the boy's shoulder, the grip careful both ways: too firm for the ribs' still-mending sake, too light for the heart's.

"Kid," said Tony Stark, at the speed of a sincere man, "he ain't forgetting. Book it."

*PANEL FIVE — THE ICING*

The second flight wanted the one thing the first flight had pointed at with a finger of smoke: altitude.

He was past forty thousand feet with the coastline gone to abstraction and the thinning air wearing that Lunar pale, when JARVIS opened his campaign the way he did all serious campaigns — late, and with manners.

"Sir. As the altitude limit approaches, I feel obligated to note a flaw in this specific platform's design. At sufficient height, the suit's exterior will accumulate ice, and the ice will eventually impede power distribution. The Blackbird's old crew held the crown at eighty-five thousand. I recommend fifty-five and a warm bath."

"Unlock the ceiling, JARVIS."

"Sir—"

"I said unlock it. Let's see the crown's little brother."

Fifty. Sixty. The sky thinned to blackened blue, the curvature's rumor beginning to lean in at the edges, and the ice came around him the way the desert had once come around him — not to kill him maliciously, just to count the steps until the cold could collect. Sensor by sensor, the channels failed courteous little blurps; JARVIS's voice thinned; the suit went dead at his back — and Tony Stark fell out of the sky.

He fell the way he'd fallen before — suit-shaped, screaming telemetry, the panic commandeered into fingers — boots re-primed, reboot, primed again, the checklist running itself at the speed of a heart trying to stay growth-sized — and out over the water, with the house glimmering on the cliff like a Christmas someone owed him, the main thrusters reignited with an oath of light.

"Ignition systems returning online, sir," JARVIS said, unruffled, as if reporting the arrival of the morning papers. "Welcome back."

"That was — *hnnh* — " the ground was rushing up uninvited — "not optimal—"

He hit the roof of the Malibu house with the ceramic crash of the world's best tile-breaker, dropped through the maintenance gap that the architects had nobody to blame for, and ended, through a storm of splinters and ivory, standing broadside on the parlor's Steinway, which had not, up until tonight, been load-bearing.

The sound was the parlor's whole inheritance coming due. BANG. Strings surrendering their souls through the slab. Above, in his room, Mark sat bolt upright in bed with the textbook flat on his chest, diary against his thigh, trembling in the frozen moment's calm.

"JARVIS."

"Sir," JARVIS said — to Mark, courtesy at full dress, in the manner of a retainer reciting his formal family's official story — "a scheduled resonance-calibration for the balcony's sonic-balance systems. Apologies for the noise. The manufacturer measures its own accuracy by volume."

Mark stared at the ceiling for a long moment with the practiced skepticism of a boy raised by men with secrets.

Then he opened the notebook. *Dad's projects have percussion. The Steinway is injured. Will investigate at breakfast, through entirely earthly means.*

Downstairs in the parlor, the suit stood very still on the piano for a moment, as if listening to make sure nobody was home to see it — and JARVIS, courteous to the last, logged nothing.

*PANEL SIX — HOT-ROD RED*

The workshop kept one channel of news running all week by standing library, the way the workshops of empire do, the sound pared down to pained murmur while the practical work went on around it. Stock debates. Board statements. And then, on the morning after the first coat of silver dried — silver, the sober draftman's draft, chrome with something still to argue for — the segment it could not fail to carry finally arrived.

The small province around Kunar, the feed said. The town called Gulmira.

There was the village square, footage-taken through flames: women running with children the way grain runs when sacks break; men hauled kneeling toward their own doorsteps; the uncovered silver sweep of ordnance shells under desert sun — sleek, efficient, stamped —

— stamped with his family name.

Tony stood at the workbench and did not stop working. That was the horror of it, the thing Pepper would later say to a board member she didn't like: you could read all of him in the way he did not stop working. His jaw set like poured slag. The shell-head in his hands turned with the patience of a clock factory. The news volume never rose; it was the air around the noise that thickened, until the workshop itself felt sectioned off from the rest of the planet's grammar, until the Yinsen words he had been carrying for five months climbed back out: *don't waste it.*

"JARVIS," he said. "Throw a little hot rod red in there."

The silver got a coat of blood-warm red the color of an election poster at the end of the campaign. It dried under the shop's gold lighting, armature by armature, gauntlet by gauntlet, until the figure on the rack looked less like a machine and more like a vow. Nobody asked whose name would fly it — Mark did not have to be told: the racks took the family name, always, that was law now, and the red suit on its stand was just the first time the public would ever be handed evidence of what fathers did with names.

Mark walked through on the way to breakfast on the third morning and stopped at the threshold, drawn by something he could feel more than identify: the hush had a grain to it now, the grain of big decisions going down whole. The red suit was gleamed atop its cage; his father was washing brushes with the long thoughtful silence of a man throwing things into a river and building an ark.

"Friday?" Mark asked.

"Friday," Tony said automatically, which meant law. "Import the pickle jar, you're chief of staff."

He didn't know yet where else any of this was headed. The news was firewalled at his side of the house, as designed. But even by human means he could watch the backs of the wrists, watch the attention test gravity, and he marked it in the notebook without facts, because it stood by rules of family even without them: *Whatever he does tonight, it's the kind out of caves.*

*PANEL SEVEN — THE STEWARDSHIP OF A GOOD GOWN*

The benefit came toward the calendar the way storms come toward mountains: through arrangement of charities across the city, through Pepper's office traffic, through the boiled-down intention that philanthropy — where the shirts were very nice, where the floor was very polished — should for one reassured evening look like it believed in Stark Industries' conscience. The annual gala for the fire department's families had arrived at its day.

Pepper had spent the week, among other sundries, holding the front desk of a war against underdevelopment, malfeasance, speculation, and one federal agency whose representative kept appearing in pay parking rooms in weirdly nice suits. The man was waiting by the executive ramp on Thursday evening as she on her way out, balanced on the balls of his feet like a scale, watching the lot like a hawk in a sport coat.

"Miss Potts."

"Phil," she said, and walked faster.

"Phil Coulson," he said, catching up with a matched stride of politeness. "From the Strategic Homeland Intervention, Enforcement and Logistics Division."

She stopped walking at the complete plague of it. "That's a mouthful."

"We're working on it," he said, evenly, with what she had privately catalogued the first time as *Mayberry sincerity with a government email.*

"Let me guess," Pepper said. "You're here to reschedule the debriefing he's declined, declined, and declined."

"I'd appreciate the opportunity to put him in a room with our analysts. Debriefings are how we keep things safe." The sincerity went up a polite increment. "You'd help him best by helping him through the door."

"You'd help him best," Pepper said, and her voice had gone to the stainless register she kept for men who thought professional fait accompli opened her drawers, "by standing in fewer parking structures. My boss has a son, an shareholders meeting, an arms embargo he just sent the entire world into shock over, and — " she started moving again, cool in the worn heels she kept at the office for nights like these, "— a schedule that has no room for acronyms until next Tuesday. Talk to my desk in the morning."

"Tuesday," Coulson echoed, and wrote nowhere down, because men of his station didn't need the paper. "Thank you, Miss Potts."

"That's a courtesy, Phil. It's not a date."

He watched her pull out with the still patience of a man who had gotten exactly what he had come for: Tuesday.

Saturday arrived on wire wheels. The dress Pepper had tried on in the home mirror downstairs on Thursday, with the aid of the sewing mirror lent by the apartment's long reflection — backless, rainy-sky blue — had managed the week; she stepped out the front door in it as the first stars showed up for the coast, and Tony, at the ignition end of the car lineup already running, squashed both eyes like a camera framing something he hadn't commissioned.

"Well," he said. "Somebody got there before I could."

"Get in the car, Tony."

"I'm saying: that's an endorsement of myself as an event planner. You *showed up.*"

"Get in the car."

He got in the car, and the coast's evening closed like a drawn curtain around the house behind them. Mark watched from the second-floor terrace, arms set on the rail, unnoticed by either and noticed by both — the way you wave to someone in the audience from a boat that has just decided it would rather be a wheelbarrow on land. His father's hand came up out the open window — *his* farewell, the one that had been running airports since birth — and Pepper waved back at him as the car pulled out.

The gala was on. And Mark Stark, as ever, was twelve, on the last rung of bedtime, writing.

*PANEL EIGHT — THE HALL OF DONORS*

The hall took its attire seriously: a cathedral's bones under a gala's jewelry, red-roped entry, sponsors layered into tiers, the whole night seizing on elegance's best handshake. Tony left Phil Coulson — introduced at the door again with the full divisional name, offering him a Tuesday — mid-chirp of enthusiasm while Pepper led him to the floor with the dais-adjacent calm of somebody who'd field-positioned people under prettier ceilings.

"Phil Coulson," said the suit, hands together like an altar promising. "Strategic Homeland Intervention, Enforcement and Logistics Division. Mr. Stark — I'd like—"

"—Tuesday," Tony said, past him already. "He told me. That's great. Stick with Pepper. She owns the calendar—"

— and the floor opened, and the violin mounted, and the donors swarmed, and for two hours the gala did what galas do at the center of an empire: wine glasses progressed; ambassadors of household-account philanthropy exchanged quorum's side-eye; a chaff of senators, newspapermen, stockmen stepped in counterfeit comfort. The applause for the evening's chairman was gracious and feverish in equal measure.

When Tony found Pepper again, near the edge of the dance floor, he arrived with the not-quite-committed poise of a man choosing the easier of two reprisals.

"Dance," he said.

"Tony."

"I don't see why not." He held out his hand.

"I don't dance," she said, firm-first, and then — watching the options — "—badly."

"You know one of the things I know about you?" He took her hand, and the crowd carried them into the light. "You've lied about that every year. You dance fine."

She did — curve for curve, step for step, circle after circle under the chandelier's storm. She topped every move he offered, her spine against his palm like the wires of something that had been humming since before the boardroom. The floor's bystanders began noticing first the red of the dress and then the way chronology got bent around them; the gossip's teeth were already working the story of *with-* and *her-* over the rim of every glass.

At the end of the song he bowed, and — around the distance between her knuckles and the little spark of contact — the rumor delivered itself to the bar before the song was out: he'd kissed her hand, palm-up, like a man posing for a portrait he hadn't commissioned.

"Air," Tony said. "Outside. I don't love close talk in expensive rooms."

She let him walk her out — past the staff pinning little notes on the balconies' frames, past the coatroom girls who'd remember every shoulder in the room come Monday, out onto the long stone terrace where the valley fog came up softly over the railings and the wind smelled heatless and expensive. Couples strolled and murmured beyond the doors. The stars came, one by one, through the smogged shield of the yard.

And a blonde intercepted them at the balustrade with all the reassured poise of a reporter who has cornered history in a moment of wealth.

"Mr Stark. *Vanity Fair.*"

Tony's face did the thing she had catalogued early: the performance mask sliding, the man's actual face underneath. "Christine. As ever: in-line."

"Everhart," she said, and the handbag opened onto a sheaf of photographic paper. "My father used to tell me never to follow a big-money man into his own charity function. He didn't warn me what to do if my big-money man's product showed up in a village called Gulmira, wrapped around some of your — *stardust.*"

She didn't wait for permission to run the interview standing up. The photographs she laid on the stone — the coarse gloss of war photography — sun-wash, sun-stricken, an explosion's aftermath tucked into a street: torn wheels, torn men, and lying against a broken wall in the foreground, a missile shell with his family's serial —

Tony's hand went out before his grammar did. "Where did you get these."

"On the ground. In the field." She watched him with the whetted wariness journalists get when millions of dollars are listening. "Ten Rings march into this little town every season, Mr Stark — using *your* product. Did you tell any of your shareholders that?"

The valley fog kept coming up. The wind had stopped smelling expensive. Tony looked at the photographs and the photographs looked back with the frozen accuracy of evidence everywhere at once — and something crossed his face that Christine Everhart, to her credit, read as her story leaving the vault and alone going cold on her claim.

"I don't have a quote for you tonight," Tony said.

"Always figured you'd want to give one. The chest—" She made the gun-portrait of a pen at his lapel. "First they name the unit after the desert, then—"

"Not tonight."

He handed the sheaf back with the quiet precision of a man who has decided on his itinerary and pocketed the photographs of it instead. The evening parted around him as he walked back in — past the donors, past Pepper's doubled-back alarm, past the little knot of men in the corner who'd already gone to phones.

Pepper caught his sleeve at the door. "Tony, what is it — where are you going—"

"To build better," he said softly. "Keep my seat warm for me, Pep."

The car went down the hill before the speculation could encircle the vale; the night felt it — the shift in pressure was rural-quiet, the kind newsrooms would still be taking apart in the morning — and at the cliff house, in the workshop below the rooms that had allowed slight light to show Friday night open, the red suit was already downward from the rack when the engine hit the driveway.

*PANEL NINE — ENTRY TWENTY-FOUR*

Midnight wore itself thin over the water. Mark had been told to bed, and gone, and given the floor to the rumors — the soft processional machinery of JARVIS' evening discipline — and stayed up under the blanket's passport with a flashlight's little borrowed courage and four minutes of notebook work.

*Entry twenty-four.*

*Friday was great. Dad came home late and the steak-and-cheeseburgers crew was full. He was distracted — eyes going inward — same look the conference news gave him. He went out tonight, in a tux, and he didn't come home yet, and the car hasn't run up the hill.*

*I don't know what it is. The television tells me it's Kunar-related. The notebooks tell me it's Dad-related — standing-fast, watching-attention thing. The room tells me he's had enough coffee.*

*I will not go looking with anything beyond human means tonight. The balance of power in his chest is mine to leave unopened.*

He set the pencil down. Put the notebook away. Then, because this was the room's ritual now, in this house, since the tritium lamp's small-and-faithful watch had taken up its shelf above the desk since what felt like forever, he whispered under the covers like a boy whose father had promised sandwich lunches all over this earth:

"Good night, Dad."

The lamp watched with its small cold steadiness. JARVIS, in the walls, kept the porter's night-shift over all of their secrets — the suit's red registration headed south over the ocean, the boy's entry sealed in a notebook, the plaque on the bench — *Proof that Tony Stark has a heart* — its three words unmoving through the dark of the room where both halves of that dawning life — father in the sky, son in the notebook — were working at being alive.

"Remember sandwiches," Mark finished, and slept.
